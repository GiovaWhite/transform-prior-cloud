# Frustration of H-Bonding and Frustrated Packings in a Hexamorphic Crystal System with Reversible Crystal–Crystal Transitions

DOI: 10.1021/jacs.4c05559

## Abstract

AbstractThe study of transitions between polymorphic phases is a less investigated chapter of the widely studied book of polymorphism. In this paper, we discuss the phase behavior of a new compound that has been rationally designed to show frustration of H-bonds for the strong amide N–H donor, which cannot be involved in H-bonding nor in van der Waals interactions. The compound (ImB) is a showcase of almost all possible cases of transitions between polymorphs [monotropic/enantiotropic, fast/slow, diffusive/displacive, and single-crystal-to-single-crystal (SCSC)] and of relation between polymorphs with different Z′. Six crystal phases (I, II, III, IV, V, and VI) were identified for it with five crystal–crystal transitions. Two transitions are reversible/SCSC/fast. Of the three monotropic transitions, all non-SCSC, one is slow, and the others are fast. Of the two enantiotropic SCSC transitions, one does not exhibit undercooling, while the other shows strong undercooling. Phase III, with Z′ = 6, is stable at room temperature between phase II (Z′ = 1), stable at high temperature, and phase IV (Z′ = 2), stable at low temperature. All six polymorphs are based on the same O–H···O=C H-bonding synthon, which produces infinite chains in five polymorphs and ring tetramers in one. The sequence of reversible SCSC transitions IV ⇆ III ⇆ II involves a remarkable ping pong of the symmetry rules by which H-bonded chains are built. Based on all of this, a possible roadmap for prediction of SCSC transitions in crystals is shortly outlined.

## Introduction

Polymorphs are different crystal structures of the same chemical substance.1 Polymorphism is relevant for applications of crystals in materials science. In fact, different crystal structures can induce completely different mechanical and electronic properties, as the archetypal example of graphite and diamond polymorphs of carbon clearly shows. In the realm of polymorphism, the issues related to transitions between polymorphic phases (monotropic/enantiotropic and slow/fast) are also relevant, and this is especially true when the transition takes place near room temperature.
Transitions between polymorphs can be classified into two types: displacive and diffusive.2−4 Displacive transitions are also named diffusionless, while diffusive ones are also named reconstructive. Displacive transitions involve small movements of molecules from one phase to the other, while in diffusive transitions, molecular movements are relevant. In diffusionless transitions, the small movements of molecules can be compatible with the preservation of macroscopic single crystals during the transition. In this case, transitions are designated as single-crystal-to-single-crystal transitions (SCSC). SCSC transitions are remarkable because they involve strongly correlated microscopic movements of individuals (atoms/molecules) over macroscopic distances. They are unique examples in which sub-nanometric displacements of all the molecules of a given macroscopic set (the single crystal) are realized collectively, coherently, and spontaneously. This allows the amplification of the microscopic molecular motions to produce dynamic macroscopic effects, such as crystal reshaping (lengthening, shrinkage twisting, and bending), crystal hopping, and crystal bursting.5 This translation of microscopic molecular movements into macroscopic body motion is the challenging issue in the development of mechanical molecular devices.6
The study of these dynamic crystals, as they are also named, is increasing, due to the potentialities they offer in the field of high-performance smart materials as soft and lightweight actuators.7−9
The most attractive dynamic crystals are those showing reversible SCSC transitions.5,10−13 As an added value, they could be used for energy storage or delivery.
However, there is no unfailing recipe for inducing polymorphism and a fortiori polymorphism with SCSC transitions. Indeed, there are examples of compounds with several polymorphic forms and no or few solid-state transitions between polymorphs.1
It has been suggested that molecules with multiple H-bonding donor/acceptor sites can have propensity to polymorphism.1 In fact, the strength and directionality of H bonds are such that different H bonding patterns between donor and acceptor groups can be realized in different low energy packings.1 The number of potential low energy packings is also increased by the capability of some groups to act as bifurcated (or multi-furcated) donors/acceptors. However, the preferential formation of H-bond between the strongest donor and acceptor groups1 reduces the degrees of freedom for packing and lowers the propensity to polymorphism.
This preference is no longer valid in the case of H-bonding frustration. This refers to situations in which functional groups capable of H-bonding fail to establish any donor–acceptor interaction in the crystal.14−19 There can be several factors responsible for frustration, for instance, the mismatch of the number of H-bond donors and acceptors, or the inaccessibility of a donor (or acceptor) site of a molecule for structural reasons.
We have found in imines of 4-hydroxybenzohydrazide with aliphatic ketones, Chart 1, a class of compounds with some propensity to polymorphism.10,20−22 In these compounds, there are two H-bonding donors (amido NH and phenolic OH) and two acceptors (carbonyl O and imino N).
One potential factor contributing to polymorphism is the phenolic donor, which, in the cases we have studied so far, can take two different conformations in the plane of the phenyl: in the same side of the carbonyl acceptor (Ha) or in the opposite side (Hb).10,20−22 Another factor is that the two acceptors are only three bonds apart, so, depending on small structural variations in the molecule, they can act as acceptors from the same (bifucated) donor. Amido NH is always a donor only to carbonyl O. Phenolic OH is a donor to imino N, but in some cases, it is a bifurcated donor both to imino N and carbonyl O, the latter being a bifurcated acceptor.
In this paper, we present a remarkable variation on this theme, shown in Chart 2, and based on the idea of frustrating the strong amido NH donor.
In ImB, in which benzophenone is condensed with 4-hydroxybenzohydrazide, amido NH is in a pocket delimited by the ortho hydrogen of ring A and by ring C, and thus, for any reasonable packing, it is hardly accessible by any acceptor group of close molecules, whatever their orientation be.

## Results and Discussion

## Thermal and Morphological Analysis
ImB has rich polymorphic behavior. Altogether, we have identified and fully characterized six crystal phases, named I, II, III, IV, V, and VI, with five crystal–crystal transitions. The different polymorphs were obtained by following the procedures described in Supporting Information. The relationships between the various phases are shown in Chart 3, while representative DSC curves are reported in Figure 1 (and Supporting Information). It must be noted that benzophenone can be considered a polymorphore23 because benzophenone alone and many substituted benzophenones are polymorphic.24
A sample of phase I transforms at 163 °C (ΔH = 2.4 kJ/mol) into a new crystal phase (phase II). In a similar way, a sample of phase V at 200 °C (ΔH = 6.2 kJ/mol) and of phase VI at 172 °C (ΔH = 14.5 kJ/mol) transforms again into phase II. By cooling phase II, phases I, V, or VI are no longer obtained, but at 104 °C, there is transition to a new crystal phase (phase III). The transition II/III is enantiotropic, and heating phase III results in phase II again (ΔH = 1.24 kJ/mol). By cooling phase III, at −16 °C, a transition to a new crystal phase is observed (phase IV). The transition III/IV is again enantiotropic, and by heating phase IV, transformation into phase III is observed at 22 °C (ΔH = 1.1 kJ/mol). Heating phase II leads to melting of ImB at 239 °C (ΔH = 31.2 kJ/mol), and cooling of the liquid phase produces phase II.
The monotropic transition V → II is diffusive/reconstructive, as shown in Figure 2. During the transition, some nucleation points of daughter phase II are observed in the parent crystal of phase V, whose phase fronts slowly advance through the crystal in different directions (movie V_II).25 As an indicative example, for the single crystal of Figure 2a, the transition was accomplished within 33 s at a 10 °C/min heating rate.
As shown in Figure 2, a single crystal of phase V, after the transition, is transformed into a sample of phase II that keeps the original dimensions and shape but contains single crystal domains with different relative orientations that extinguish at different angles under crossed polarizers.
The monotropic transition VI → II is also diffusive, but unlike V → II, it is fast (3.5 s for the specimen of Figure 3) and, after the transition, a powdered specimen of daughter phase II is obtained (movie VI_II), which retains the original dimensions and shape. At the polarizing microscope, little evidence of birefringence is observed in the final sample but only a dark field confirms the powdered nature of the sample, Figure 3.
A similar behavior is also observed for the monotropic transition I → II (movie I_II).
The (reversible) transition III → II at 104 °C is SCSC. During the transition, only one phase front is observed in the parent single crystal with a regular line geometry, which quickly advances in the parent crystal, Figure 4. There are no relevant dimensional changes of the crystal during the transformation (movie III_II and movie II_III). After the transition, a single crystal of daughter phase II is obtained.
The whole transition is accomplished within 3 s. Fast transformation and geometrically regular front phase are considered signatures of SCSC transitions.6,11,25
The reversible transition III/II is noteworthy because, as shown in the DSC thermograms, it has virtually no undercooling. The difference between the (onset) transition temperature on heating and cooling is within 0.5 °C, over a range of heating/cooling rates spanning between 0.5 and 20 °C/min (see Supporting Information). Small undercooling is typical of liquid–liquid transitions (e.g., nematic to isotropic in liquid crystals), while it is uncommon in first-order solid-state transitions. At any rate, we are not aware of any other example of crystal–crystal transition with such small undercooling.
In contrast, strong undercooling, by almost 40 °C, is observed for the enantiotropic SCSC transition III → IV. The transition from phase IV to III on heating is accompanied by a significant transversal contraction of single crystals (by about 4%; see movie IV_III), as shown in Figure 5.
## Structural Analysis
The molecular conformation of ImB is basically determined by the orientation of the two phenyl rings B and C of Chart 2 with respect to each other and to the sp2 plane of imino C carbon. Coplanarity of rings B and C is impossible because of the close contact between the inner ortho hydrogens. Coplanarity of ring C with the imino plane is strongly disfavored by the close contact of the ortho hydrogen with amido hydrogen. On the other hand, there is no intramolecular close contact involving ring B in coplanar arrangement with the imino plane. In fact, in all the structures investigated (a total of 14 independent molecules out of six polymorphs), the dihedral angle τ between ring C and imino plane ranges between 55.1(1)° and 86.8(2)°, while dihedral angle φ between ring B and imino plane ranges between 2.9(1)° and 30.1(2)° (see Supporting Information). The conformation of the phenolic OH is Ha (Chart 1) in all independent molecules of polymorphs I, II, III, IV, and V, while it is Hb in VI. The crystal structure of the six polymorphs of ImB has been determined by single-crystal X-ray analysis. An overview of the space groups and lattice parameters is presented in Table 1 (full details, including a discussion of different cell settings, are given in Supporting Information). Polymorphs II, III, and IV, which are related by reversible crystal–crystal transitions, are monoclinic, and the unique axis b is essentially constant over them (maximum difference is 0.126 Å, which also accounts for the different temperature of measurement). Phases I and VI are also monoclinic, with the same space group as II and IV, but lattice parameters are different and the unique axis b is different. In contrast, phase V is triclinic.
The common feature of all six polymorphs of ImB is the formation of H-bonds between the O–H donor and carbonyl O acceptor, and this is the only strong H bond found in all the crystal structures. Amido N–H donor is never involved in H-bonds, nor is it involved in any short intermolecular contact at a distance less than the sum of van der Waals radii plus 0.1 Å. Therefore, a complete frustration of N–H as H bonding donor is present in ImB. The frequency of such a complete frustration is low (7%) based on a set of 2250 hits found in CSD26 and containing the group –C(=O)–NH–N–. The common structural motif of polymorphs II, III, and IV is the formation of H-bonded chains running parallel to the unique axis b, as illustrated in Figure 6, and this accounts for the basically constant value of b axis in the three polymorphs. Given the Ha conformation of phenolic OH, it is evident that H-bonded chains cannot be generated by molecules simply related by translation. A rotation of 180° around the long axis is needed to achieve the right orientation of the OH donor and the O=C acceptor. The difference between polymorphs is in the crystallographic operations that produce the right orientation and generate the chains, as shown in Figure 6.
In the high-temperature polymorph II, chains are generated in the simplest way. They are wrapped around crystallographic screw axes and comprise the sole independent molecule of the crystal structure. In lower-temperature phase IV, chains are produced by b⃗ translation of couples of two H-bonded independent molecules (red/blue). In room-temperature polymorph III, there is an enthralling and remarkable mixture of those two ways. In fact, in III, there are six independent molecules and four different chains (Figure 6b). Two chains are generated by b⃗ translation of couples of H-bonded independent molecules (red/blue and magenta/gray), just as in polymorph IV, and the two others are wrapped around crystallographic screw axes and contain the remaining two independent molecules (orange and green), as in the high-temperature polymorph II. The complexity of the chain pattern in III may characterize this polymorph as a frustrated case along the way between the simpler II and IV patterns.27,28 Frustration is also confirmed by the observation that although the true space group of III is P21, as compared with P21/c of II and IV, this polymorph has a strong pseudocentrosymmetric character, so it is quasi P21/c,29 as it is also evident in Figure 6b by the (pseudo) centrosymmetric arrangement of orange and magenta/gray chains with respect to green and red/blue chains. It is a matter of reflection why the simpler (Z′ = 1) structure II is not retained at room (phase III, Z′ = 6) and low (phase IV, Z′ = 2) temperatures.30,31 Values of crystal density (Table 1) can suggest that (vibrational) entropy coupled with the awkward molecular shape31 plays a role.
Chains are also present in polymorphs I and VI but, in this case, they are perpendicular to b⃗ and run along and c⃗, respectively, Figure 7. In I, chains are formed by translation of couples of H-bonded independent molecules (blue/red), whereas in VI, chains are formed by glide reflection of the independent molecule. Moreover, in VI, the conformation of phenolic OH in the independent molecule is Hb, while in all other polymorphs, it is Ha.
As a matter of fact, the conditions met in I, II, III, IV, and VI, namely, chains parallel or perpendicular to b are the only ones allowing chains to be parallel to each other in a monoclinic crystal. Moreover, these polymorphs also illustrate possible symmetries of a “polymer” chain containing two molecules in the chain axis.32
In triclinic phase V, a completely different arrangement is present. The two independent molecules are almost orthogonal to each other, and the usual O–H···O=C synthon produces closed tetramers instead of chains as in the other four polymorphs, Figure 7c. This is consistent with the diffusive/reconstructive nature and slowness of transition V → II, as previously discussed.
An insightful comparison of polymorphs II, III, and IV can be performed by finding a common unit cell for them. A suitable supercell common to the three polymorphs is found for Z = 24 and the same value of b. Table 2 shows that the transition of phase III to II at 104 °C on heating produces small increases of a′ and c′ axis (1.4 and 0.2%, respectively), and this is consistent with the macroscopic features of the transition previously discussed (Figure 4 and movies III_II and II_III). The transition from phase IV to III on heating involves a small contraction of the b axis (−0.8%) and comparatively major deformations of the (a′,c′) net, with 4% contraction of the a′ axis, and this is also consistent with the macroscopic features of the transition.
The common features of the packings of II, III, and IV are evident when examining the supercells shown in Figure 8. The H-bonded chains form undulated layers parallel to the plane (b′,c′). Within each layer, chains, which are parallel to unique axis b′, are spaced by c′/2, and the layers are stacked along a′ with spacing a′/6. This strong metric similarity is consistent with the nondiffusive/displacive nature of the transitions between phases II, III, and IV. In phase II, each layer is formed by chains having screw symmetry (S-type layer), and so, along a′, there is a ···SSS··· sequence of layers. In IV, each layer is formed by chains having translational symmetry (layer T), and so the sequence of layers is ···TTT···. In III, of the four independent H-bonded chains, two have screw symmetry and two have translational symmetry. The chains are arranged in S- and T-type layers that, along a′, are disposed in ordered ···STT··· sequence. So, these reversible phase transitions are noteworthy, as they feature a remarkable ping-pong between chain symmetries and pseudosymmetries (see Supporting Information).
The analysis of linear thermal expansion coefficients (see Supporting Information) indicates that phases IV and III have one very high (colossal33,34) thermal expansion coefficient (235 × 10–6 K–1 and 208 × 10–6 K–1, respectively) along a principal axis whose major component (99%) is along crystallographic c axis for phase IV and a axis for phase III, and so in a direction basically transverse with respect to the undulated layers of Figure 8. This is similar to what we already observed in another case of SCSC transition between stacked-layer structures.6 The pattern of thermal expansion coefficients may suggest a mechanism of the SCSC transitions III/II and IV/III, in which there is a loosening in the stacking of the undulated layers and, in the case of the transition IV/III, also a parallel shift of the layers.25 Colossal thermal expansion coefficients might be another signature of SCSC transitions.6,12,13
Raman spectra of phases II, III, and IV are shown in Figure 9.
The spectra were recorded from the same single crystal heated and cooled across the three phases. As expected, there is strong similarity among the spectra for Raman shifts greater than 200 cm–1, which corresponds to the region of internal crystal lattice vibrational modes (see Supporting Information).35 External crystal lattice vibrational modes, which are generally the most sensitive to differences in the crystal packing and the most useful for differentiating polymorphs, fall in the region of Raman shifts between 10 and 200 cm–1.35 In this region, the spectra of the three polymorphs are different, with the spectrum of phase IV showing the most relevant differences. This is consistent with the results of the crystallographic analysis.

The stacking of layers shows a remarkable example of Kitaigorodskii’s “bumps in hollows” principle,32 and this phase, with Z′ = 1, has the highest density and highest ΔH of transition among all polymorphs of ImB (Table 1), though being metastable with respect to the triad II/III/IV. Looking at Table 1, we see that the stable phase III (Z′ = 6) at room temperature is the one with lowest density if compared with metastable phases I (Z′ = 2), V (Z′ = 2), and VI (Z′ = 1).

## Conclusions

The combined pattern of polymorphism and crystal–crystal transitions of ImB hitherto described (six polymorphs, five transitions) is very rich and perhaps record setting.36−38
This polymorphism is not related to dramatic conformational changes of the molecule in the various phases. It can be related instead with the polymorphore nature of the benzophenone moiety and with the complete H-bonding frustration of the strong amide NH donor that leaves the H···O=C bond as the sole possible H-bonding synthon. Remarkably, it is found that this synthon can be realized in six different phases, related to five crystal–crystal transitions. Of these, two are SCSC enantiotropic and three are monotropic non-SCSC.
In first-order phase transitions, there is no relation, in principle, between the symmetry of the phases involved in the transition.39 However, one may wonder if in the specific set of nondiffusive SCSC transitions, some general conditions must be met.40 Reversible SCSC transitions occur in ImB between monoclinic phases II and III and between III and IV. Phases II/III/IV share two common features: (1) they are characterized by strong metric similarity in the position of the chains, as proven by the existence of common supercells; (2) the orientation of supramolecular structures (H-bonded chains) with respect to crystallographic symmetry elements (the unique b axis) is kept. On the other hand, in monoclinic phases I/II and VI/II, the H-bonded chains are differently oriented with respect to the unique axis, and transitions I → II and VI → II are not SCSC.
In the hypothesis that conditions (1) and (2) be generally valid, they could be used to develop a roadmap for predicting SCSC transitions. In fact, the set of predicted crystal structures of a given compound,41 suitably ranked in order of lattice energy, could be automatically screened, at first, for the existence of common supercells.42 The subset obtained in this way could be further screened for the existence of structures that keep the orientation of relevant supramolecular architectures (e.g., chains, ribbons, and dimers) with respect to symmetry elements.
