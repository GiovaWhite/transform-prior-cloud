# Exploring Polymorphism: Hydrochloride Salts of Pitolisant and Analogues

DOI: 10.1021/acs.cgd.3c01245

## Abstract

Pitolisant hydrochloride is used to treat excessive daytime sleepiness in adults with narcolepsy.
The drug is formulated as a crystalline solid, and a monoclinic P21 form has been claimed in
patents, but little additional information about the structure and polymorphism of the compound
has been published. No new forms were obtained when we grew crystals from solution under
various conditions. Reexamination of the crystals revealed a disordered and partially hydrated
structure that resembles the one reported earlier but is not identical. Further insight was obtained
by synthesizing analogues of pitolisant with its Cl substituent replaced by Me, F, and Br, followed
by structural analysis of the hydrochloride salts by X-ray diffraction. Pitolisant hydrochloride and
its three analogues showed very similar solid-state behavior, and each compound yielded new
metastable forms when crystallized from melts. The lifetime of metastable Form III of pitolisant
hydrochloride could be extended significantly by adding small amounts of the fluoro analogue,
but none of the metastable forms could be obtained as single crystals suitable for structural
analysis. Computational predictions of the polymorphic landscapes of pitolisant hydrochloride and
its analogues identified possible structures of the metastable forms. Dual experimental and
computational approaches are already widely used in polymorphic screening, but our work shows
the value of broadening these searches to include sets of structural analogues.

## Introduction

The hydrochloride salt of pitolisant (1 • HCl) is a first-in-class drug used to treat excessive daytime
sleepiness in adults with narcolepsy.1 The compound acts as an antagonist/inverse agonist of the
histamine-3 (H3) receptor, and it appears to activate histaminergic neurons in the brain that enhance
wakefulness. The drug was approved for medical use in oral form by the U. S. Food and Drug
Administration in 2019. The product, marketed as Wakix by Harmony Biosciences, achieved
annual sales of $440 M in fiscal year 2022.
Pitolisant hydrochloride is formulated as a crystalline solid, but little information about its
structure and polymorphism has been published. A monoclinic P21 form has been claimed in
patents,2,3 but the structural analysis is complicated by the presence of disorder and variable
degrees of hydration. We have reexamined the reported structure and searched for other solid
forms using standard methods of polymorphic screening, as well as newer targeted approaches
based on heteroseeding with both pure and mixed crystals of related compounds.4−12
Simultaneously, we have used computational methods of crystal structure prediction (CSP) to
assess the behavior of pitolisant hydrochloride and selected analogues. Together, this work has
provided new information about the polymorphism of pitolisant hydrochloride and compounds
like it. More generally, our results have yielded a deeper understanding of how the polymorphic
3
landscape of active pharmaceutical ingredients and other compounds of special interest can be
explored systematically, how new solid forms can be created, and how the lifetimes of metastable
forms can be extended.

## Results and Discussion

Structure of Form I of Pitolisant Hydrochloride. In about 1000 experiments, we crystallized
pitolisant hydrochloride from solution under various conditions, but we found no form other than
one similar to the reported P21 structure.2,3 These experiments used pitolisant hydrochloride of at
least 99% chemical purity, as obtained from commercial suppliers or prepared ourselves by a
published route.13−15 Routine screening did not yield a new form but provided satisfactory ways to
grow single crystals of pitolisant hydrochloride for structural analysis by X-ray diffraction.
In the previously reported study of pitolisant hydrochloride,2,3 crystals were grown from ethyl
acetate/isopropanol. The nature of the variable degree of hydration of crystals obtained under these
conditions was not fully described. We found that when diisopropyl ether was carefully layered
over solutions of the salt in dioxane, slow interdiffusion led to the growth of characteristic thin
platelets that proved to belong to the monoclinic space group P21. The resulting structure (Form I
of salt 1 • HCl) resembles the one described earlier but is not identical. Table 1 summarizes
crystallographic data, and Figures 1 and 2 provide views of the structure. Samples prepared under
our conditions were essentially phase-pure, as established by comparing the simulated powder X-
ray diffraction pattern with those obtained experimentally by analyzing bulk samples.16

Pitolisant hydrochloride is an amphiphilic molecule with an ionic piperidinium head and nonionic
arylalkyl tail. In Form I, individual molecules adopt conformations that are partially extended
along the c-axis (Figure 1), and packing creates regions of alternating ionic and nonionic character.
The observed structure is a monohydrate in which molecules of H2O occupy isolated pockets lying
along the a-axis. Data summarized in Table 1 were obtained by analyzing a crystal in which 58%

showed losses of weight during heating from 25 to 150 °C that are consistent with partial
occupancy of the site of hydration.16
Analysis of the Form I structure of pitolisant hydrochloride is complicated not only by partial
occupancy, but also by disorder arising from the incorporation of two conformations (Figure 2).
In the previously reported structural analysis (summarized in column 3 of Table 1),2,3 the
asymmetric unit was considered to have Z' = 2 and to comprise two conformations (Figure 2a),
one with a torsional angle C3–C2–C1–O of 172° (trans conformation A) and the other with an
angle of 74° (gauche conformation B). The two conformations are superimposed in Figure 2b to
show that the positions of the ionic head and nonionic tail remain essentially unchanged. Our data
support an alternative structural model for Form I, in which Z' = 1 and the asymmetric unit consists
of overlapping conformations of types A and B, with torsional angles C3–C2–C1–O of 167° and
89°, respectively.
Crystallization of Form I appears to occur rapidly by a process of assembly in which molecules of
H2O are frequently omitted. Each included molecule of H2O forms three noteworthy interactions:
(1) an O–H···O hydrogen bond with the oxygen atom of pitolisant hydrochloride (dO···O = 2.880
Å); (2) an O–H···Cl hydrogen bond with a chloride ion associated primarily with another
molecule of pitolisant (dO···Cl = 3.257 Å); and (3) a short C–Haryl···O interaction (dO···H = 2.276

Structures of the Hydrochloride Salts of Me-, F-, and Br-Substituted Analogues 2–4 of
Pitolisant. To better understand the complex solid-state behavior of pitolisant hydrochloride, we
synthesized a set of analogues, crystallized them, and solved their structures. The new compounds
included the hydrochloride salts of Me-, F-, and Br-substituted analogues 2, 3, and 4, respectively.
In addition to using these compounds to provide insights about pitolisant hydrochloride, we were
optimistic that the analogues could yield potential heteroseeds for inducing pitolisant
hydrochloride to crystallize in new ways. Compounds 2–4 were made by routes based on a recently

reaction of piperidine with 1-bromo-3-chloropropane.14,20 The corresponding hydrochloride salts

The hydrochloride salts of pitolisant and Me-, F-, and Br-substituted analogues 2–4 were
crystallized to allow their structures to be compared, to identify shared behavior, and to thereby
help develop a broader understanding of molecular crystallization. In all four cases, the following
key features were observed: (1) Crystallizations from solution under multiple conditions yielded
only one solid form of each salt; (2) the cationic components of the salts are highly flexible but
nevertheless adopt similar partially extended conformations (Figure 4); (3) the structures have
segregated regions of ionic and nonionic character; and (4) thin platelets are formed by all four
compounds. Indexing P21 crystals of Form I of pitolisant hydrochloride established that growth is
slowest along the c-axis, corresponding to the direction in which the cations are extended.
Isostructural P21 crystals of the salts of Me- and Br-substituted analogues 2 and 4 appear to show
the same preference. Side-by-side packing of conformationally extended cations may be faster
than end-to-end assembly because the surface area of molecular contact is maximized.25 Because
pockets for accommodating molecules of H2O in Form I crystals of pitolisant hydrochloride are
created when cations are packed side-by-side, rapid growth along the a- and b-axes may also help
explain why full hydration is not observed. Growth of the non-isostructural P21/c crystals of the
hydrochloride salt of fluoro analogue 3 is again slowest along the c-axis, and the cations are
conformationally extended along the ac-diagonal. The similar behavior of all four compounds
16
provides a foundation for anticipating how new analogues of pitolisant hydrochloride will
crystallize when other structural alterations are made.
Mixed Crystallization of the Hydrochloride Salts of Pitolisant and Me-, F-, and Br-
Substituted Analogues 2–4. The tendency of the hydrochloride salts of pitolisant and its Me-, F-,
and Br-substituted analogues 2–4 to crystallize in similar ways suggested that they might form
mixed crystals with continuously variable compositions. Current understanding of mixed
crystallization is based in part on pioneering studies carried out by Kitaigorodsky and others,26,27
which indicated that the phenomenon is limited to closely related compounds that prefer to
crystallize isostructurally. In fact, mixed crystals with variable compositions can be formed by a
much wider range of components, and the phenomenon is attracting renewed interest for many
reasons. 5–10,21,28–43 For example, mixed crystals provide novel ordered materials with properties
that can be adjusted continuously, simply by altering the ratio of the components.
Testing the ability of the hydrochloride salts of pitolisant and fluoro analogue 3 to form mixed
crystals is an objective of particular interest, because the two compounds are not known to
crystallize isostructurally, despite extensive polymorphic screening. As a result, they are not
expected to yield mixed crystals with widely varying compositions, according to the
generalizations of Kitaigorodsky. The two compounds were dissolved together in dioxane in
varying ratios, and crystallization was induced by carefully adding a layer of diisopropyl ether and
allowing slow mixing to occur. This yielded characteristic thin platelets, which were analyzed by
single-crystal X-ray diffraction. The results of the study are summarized in Table 2, which shows
how the composition and crystallographic parameters depend on the initial ratio of components.

Table 2 shows that the hydrochloride salts of pitolisant and fluoro analogue 3 can form mixed
crystals with a wide range of compositions, even though the two compounds have not been
observed to crystallize isostructurally. In related studies of mixed crystallizations,5,6 different
samples selected from the same batch have been shown to have similar compositions, and the
ratios of components in different parts of individual crystals have also revealed only modest
variations. Table 2 reveals that the composition of mixed crystals of the hydrochloride salts of
pitolisant and fluoro analogue 3 tends to reflect the initial ratio of components in solution, thereby
suggesting that the batches of crystals are generally uniform. Data in Table 2 show that mixed
crystals favor monoclinic P21 Form I of hydrated pitolisant hydrochloride when the composition
in solution is dominated by pitolisant hydrochloride; however, if pitolisant hydrochloride is the
minor component, then monoclinic P21/c Form I of the anhydrous hydrochloride salt of fluoro
analogue 3 is favored. The sites of hydration in Form I crystals of pure pitolisant hydrochloride
are not fully occupied, and partial incorporation of fluoro analogue 3 within the structure reduces
the occupancy further.
When two compounds crystallize isostructurally, mixed crystals can typically be formed with
continuously variable compositions. In a relationship known as Vegard’s law,44−46 the unit-cell
parameters of the resulting mixed crystals often vary linearly as the ratio of the components
changes. In certain cases, linear relationships of this type have been noted in mixed molecular
19
crystals of non-isostructural pairs.5,6,47 However, mixed P21/c crystals of the hydrochloride salts
of pitolisant and fluoro analogue 3 do not show this behavior.
The hydrochloride salts of pitolisant, methyl analogue 2, and bromo analogue 4 are known to
crystallize isostructurally (Table 1), so we expected them to form mixed crystals with widely
varying compositions. Diisopropyl ether was layered onto dioxane solutions containing different
ratios of the salts of pitolisant and methyl analogue 2, the resulting thin platelets were analyzed by
single-crystal X-ray diffraction, and the collected data are summarized in Table 3. Mixed crystals
were formed, as expected, but they showed a preference for compositions close to 1:1, even when
markedly different ratios were present in solution. We suspect that constraints are imposed on the
range of possible compositions because pure pitolisant hydrochloride crystallizes as a partially
occupied hydrate, whereas the salt of methyl analogue 2 is anhydrous. Whatever the origin of the
phenomenon, our observations underscore the inadequacies of current generalizations as a basis
for understanding mixed crystallization.

during experiments in which samples of pitolisant hydrochloride (initially present as powdered
Form I) were placed on a sheet of Kapton, assayed by PXRD at 25 °C, melted, held briefly at a

Under these conditions, no decomposition of the salt was noted, and a new polymorph (Form II)
appeared near 75 °C, quickly followed by conversion to Form III near 70 °C, and then by eventual
regeneration of Form I. Similar observations were made when Kapton was replaced by other
surfaces. Warming Form I from 25 °C to just below its melting point did not lead to significant

We were initially concerned that preferred orientations might prevent reliable analyses of the
PXRD patterns of films obtained by crystallizing pitolisant hydrochloride from melts. However,
films of Form I produced from melts gave patterns similar to those observed for powdered
crystalline samples. It was impossible for us to take films of unstabilized Forms II and III off the
underlying Kapton and record the PXRD patterns as powdered solids without triggering
conversion into Form I.
24
Melts of the hydrochloride salts of Me-, F-, and Br-substituted analogues 2–4 behaved similarly.
VT PXRD experiments showed that new metastable polymorphs were again produced by cooling
the melts but were eventually transformed into the corresponding Form I of the three salts at 25
°C.16 Again, films of Form I produced from melts of analogues 2–4 gave PXRD patterns similar
to those observed for powdered crystalline samples. These results underscore how the solid-state
landscapes of the hydrochloride salts of pitolisant and analogues 2–4 are closely related. In all
cases, Form I crystals are more stable between 25 °C and their melting points than alternative
structures. Because similar behavior is exhibited in VT PXRD experiments by salts that produce
Form I hydrates (pitolisant and Br-substitituted analogue 4) and by those that do not (Me- and F-
substituted analogues 2 and 3), we doubt that the appearance and disappearance of metastable
Forms II and III depend critically on the presence or absence of H2O.
Form I crystals of the hydrochloride salts of Me-, F-, and Br-substituted analogues 2–4 proved to
be effective seeds for inducing crystallization of supercooled melts of pitolisant hydrochloride, but
in all cases only the known P21 Form I was produced. We also tested mixed P21/c Form I crystals
containing the hydrochloride salts of both pitolisant and fluoro analogue 3; again, however,
seeding yielded only the P21 Form I of pitolisant hydrochloride. We then used VT PXRD to study
how the behavior of pitolisant hydrochloride was affected by the intentional addition of small
amounts of the hydrochloride salts of Me-, F-, and Br-substituted analogues 2–4. Including 15%
of the hydrochloride salt of methyl analogue 2 had little effect, and the mixed melt crystallized in
the standard way, with Forms II, III, and I of pitolisant hydrochloride (admixed with the salt of
methyl analogue 2) appearing in their normal sequence at temperatures that remained essentially
unchanged. In contrast, adding 15% of the hydrochloride salt of fluoro analogue 3 had a markedly
25
inhibitory effect on regeneration of Form I. Again, Form II of pitolisant hydrochloride (admixed
with the salt of fluoro analogue 3) appeared initially and was quickly transformed into Form III,
but conversion into Form I was not complete until samples had been kept at 25 °C for more than
a day.16
When stabilized in this way with 15% of the hydrochloride salt of fluoro analogue 3, Form III of
pitolisant hydrochloride could be removed from the underlying sheet of Kapton and handled
without inducing rapid conversion into Form I. This allowed Form III to be characterized by FTIR
and Raman spectroscopy; despite multiple attempts, however, we were not able to produce melt-
grown single crystals of stabilized Form III that were large enough for structural analysis by X-
ray diffraction. Similarly, we have not been able to grow satisfactory single crystals of metastable
forms of the salts of Me-, F-, and Br-substituted analogues 2–4 from their melts under various
conditions.
Nevertheless, our observations highlight the potential of using mixed crystallization to increase
the lifetime of metastable forms of compounds of interest. Various types of impurities, often
identified serendipitously, have been found to interfere with the interconversion of solid forms.48-60
However, our work goes further by revealing unusual selectivity, with inhibition caused only by
certain compounds in sets of close structural analogues that all form mixed crystals with the
compound of interest. The special inhibitory effect of the hydrochloride salt of fluoro analogue 3
may arise because the compound is similar enough to pitolisant hydrochloride to be accommodated
within the lattice of Form III, but the tendency of the components to crystallize non-isostructurally
may interfere with subsequent regeneration of Form I.
26
Polymorphic Landscape of Pitolisant Hydrochloride. To better understand the crystallization
of hydrochloride salts of pitolisant and its Me-, F-, and Br-substituted analogues 2–4, we used CSP
to assess the polymorphic landscapes. The field of CSP has advanced dramatically in recent
years,61–65 and in favorable cases it has become possible to predict the most stable forms,
alternative structures,66,67 and their relative energies with a high degree of accuracy.68 However,
using CSP to analyze the crystallization of salts of pitolisant and its analogues is made challenging
by the potential ability of the cations to adopt a wide range of conformations, the presence of
multiple types of intermolecular interactions, and the tendency of certain compounds in the series
to form hydrates and disordered structures.
Nevertheless, we were optimistic that we could use CSP successfully by taking advantage of
existing knowledge of the structures of Form I of all four salts. Even though cations in the salts
have potentially high flexibility, only a narrow range of conformations is adopted (Figure 4). This
suggests that similar conformations may be favored by pitolisant hydrochloride in crystalline
structures other than its P21 Form I. By taking the two known conformations of pitolisant
hydrochloride in disordered Form I as fixed subunits for constructing potential crystal structures,
CSP can be used to see if the observed structure emerges as a preferred polymorph and if other
modes of packing offer accessible alternatives. Similarly, the Me, F, and Br groups in the observed
structures of the salts of analogues 2–4 can be replaced computationally by Cl to create other
potential conformations of pitolisant hydrochloride, and CSP can then lead to additional sets of
potential structures.
27
In essence, this approach uses available information about a compound of interest and a small set
of analogues to simplify, guide, and accelerate CSP by offering promising starting points for
calculations. We have tested this method by applying it in the challenging case of pitolisant
hydrochloride; in principle, however, the approach can be used to survey the polymorphism of any
compound for which a small set of relevant structural data is already available.
CSP was performed for the hydrochloride salts of pitolisant and its Me- and F-substituted
analogues 2 and 3 by using the Global Lattice Energy Explorer (GLEE) program,69 which employs
a low-discrepancy quasi-random sampling of crystal-packing variables (molecular positions,
molecular orientations, and unit-cell parameters) to uniformly explore the lattice-energy surface.
In the salts of pitolisant and its Me-substituted analogue 2, cations are disordered and adopt distinct
conformations of types A and B (Figure 4). Therefore, two sets of CSP calculations were
performed for each of these salts, with either conformation A or conformation B held rigid
throughout the initial crystal-structure generation and lattice-energy minimization. Trial crystal
structures, generated in ten common space groups, were energy-minimized until a target number
of valid structures was reached. All structures were generated with one cation and one chloride
anion per asymmetric unit, and no molecules of H2O were included in the CSP calculations. Rigid-
body lattice-energy optimizations were performed using the DMACRYS software.70 Each
structure within 60 kJ mol-1 of the global energy minimum on the CSP landscape was re-optimized
using third-order density functional tight-binding theory with self-consistent charges as
implemented in the DFTB+ program.71 Upon re-optimization, a search for matches to the
experimentally observed crystal structures was performed using the COMPACK algorithm with a
30-molecule cluster,72 ignoring molecules of H2O in the experimental crystal structures. A
28
structure was considered a match if the interatomic distances and angles between the overlapping
clusters were within tolerances of 30% and 30°, respectively (30/30 match). Additional
information about the calculations is provided in the section on Computational and Experimental
Methods.

## Conclusions

Our integrated experimental and computational study of the structures of the hydrochloride salts
of pitolisant and its analogues highlights the ability of CSP to help guide searches for solid forms,
even in challenging series of compounds where flexibility, hydration, and disorder need to be
considered. However, our work also identifies limitations of the CSP approach, as applied to
pitolisant hydrochloride and analogues. Even when calculations are limited to analyses of
38
privileged conformations, predicted landscapes include dozens of potentially accessible structures
that have not been observed experimentally, despite sustained efforts to screen for solid forms.
The overprediction of polymorphism is a known problem in CSP, and methods have recently been
proposed to reduce sets of predicted structures by using clustering based on molecular dynamics79
or Monte Carlo simulations.80 In addition, the hydrochloride salts of pitolisant and its analogues
all yield metastable Forms II and III under markedly similar conditions, but these forms are not
matched definitively by any structures on the predicted landscapes.
This suggests that the unknown structures may be disordered, crystallize in uncommon space
groups that are not sampled, have asymmetric units with Z' > 1, or incorporate conformations
unlike those observed in forms characterized by single-crystal X-ray diffraction. It is important to
note that the transformations of Form II into Form III and of Form III into Form I occur in the
solid state under mild conditions, so major changes in conformation or molecular arrangement are
unlikely to be required. At present, the structures of metastable Forms II and III of the
hydrochloride salts of pitolisant and its analogues remain unknown, and attempts to identify them
are continuing.
Our work with pitolisant hydrochloride also demonstrates the value of extending screens for new
solid forms to include studies of the behavior of related compounds. For example, characterizing
Form I of pitolisant hydrochloride is complicated by the presence of disorder and hydration,
whereas these problems are eliminated in fluoro analogue 3. When the biological activity of
analogues is equivalent, differences in the ease of producing crystalline forms that are well
characterized and likely to be thermodynamically stable can become the basis for selecting a
39
compound for further development as a drug. In addition, crystals of pure analogues, as well as
mixed crystals containing analogues and the compound of interest, provide potentially effective
heteroseeds for use in polymorphic screening. In the case of pitolisant hydrochloride,
heteroseeding has not yet led to solid forms that are otherwise unavailable, but analogues have
been shown to alter crystallization of the compound in other useful ways, such as by increasing
the lifetime of metastable forms. Dual experimental and computational approaches are already
widely used to chart polymorphic landscapes, but our results show the advantages of broadening
the scope of these analyses so that they include sets of analogous compounds.
