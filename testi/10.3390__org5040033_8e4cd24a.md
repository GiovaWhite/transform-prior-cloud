# Unexpected Products of Salicylidene-Aminoguanidine Reactions with Metal Ions—Synthesis and Structural Aspects

DOI: 10.3390/org5040033

## Abstract

Due to the promising characteristics of aminoguanidine Schiff bases, ongoing research focuses on synthesizing and characterizing different compounds of this class to establish structure–property relationships. However, the pronounced alkalinity of the aminoguanidine residue makes isolating its Schiff bases in neutral form challenging. In the reaction of salicylidene-aminoguanidine ([HL]NO3) with a strong base (NaOH), the partially neutralized product of the formula [HL]NO3∙L·H2O was obtained in the form of single crystals. This compound could be considered a cocrystal in which protonated and neutral forms of the Schiff base coexist. Furthermore, the coordinating properties of [HL]NO3 towards zinc and organotin were investigated, and instead of the expected crystals of complex compounds, a novel polymorph of the ligand was obtained. Additionally, the reaction of [HL]NO3, NH4VO3 and salicylaldehyde was carried out to achieve the condensation of the free NH2-group in the aminoguanidinium fragment, targeting a vanadium(V) complex with tetradentate ligand. However, a purely organic compound containing three salicylaldehyde residues and two imine groups, i.e., C21H18N2O3, was isolated. All the obtained compounds were characterized by elemental and spectroscopic analysis, conductometry and SC-XRD analysis. The data were compared to those of similar structures, and the results provide further insight into the properties of these compounds and their future investigation for potential usage.

## Introduction

Several decades ago, aminoguanidine was suggested as a potent drug for treating diabetic nephropathy [1]. However, since it exhibited severe side effects, its Schiff bases arose as suitable substitutes [2,3]. The research was extended further, and considering that the presence of a metal center often enhances the activity of the bioactive organic molecule [4], the coordination properties of aminoguanidine Schiff bases were thoroughly examined [5–8]. More than 50 complexes with these Schiff bases were synthesized and structurally characterized, with those with tridentate coordination of the ligand being the most common. Additionally, several complexes in which aminoguanidine residue stays protonated and is not involved in coordination were also isolated [9], and some in which the Schiff base has the role of a counter ion were also reported [10,11]. These results showed that the role of the Schiff base was found to be tunable by the presence of a deprotonating agent. These findings were crucial for establishing knowledge of the differences in the structural features of those compounds, which would lead to a better understanding of the structure–property relationship. Recent findings show that complexes with bio-compatible Schiff bases of aminoguanidine can have promising DNA binding properties and chemotherapeutic activity [12,13].
Many zinc complexes with Schiff bases of aminoguanidine were synthesized and characterized to obtain materials with promising optical properties [14–17]. Since the ligand and the zinc salts were easily accessible, it seemed important to explore the possibilities of obtaining new complexes of this kind.
In this work, we describe the different routes for obtaining two polymorphs of the Schiff base derived from aminoguanidine and salicylaldehyde nitrate salt (1a and 1b). Additionally, we outline the isolation of the cocrystal (2) isolated upon attempts to produce its neutral form, as well as the unexpected diimine product (3), which was produced during an intended template synthesis of a vanadium complex. Detailed structural characterization was performed, and the data were compared with those reported earlier. Additionally, energetic and packing features in the crystal structures of two polymorphs, 3a and 3b, were thoroughly discussed.

## Materials and Methods

All chemicals used were commercially available and of analytical reagent grade. Elemental analyses (C, H, N) of the air-dried complexes were conducted using standard micromethods at the Center for Instrumental Analyses, ICTM, Belgrade, Serbia. IR spectra were obtained using KBr pellets on a NEXUS 670 FTIR spectrophotometer (Thermo Nicolet, Waltham, MA, USA) in the 4000–400 cm−1 range. Melting points were determined using a Nagema Rapido melting point microscope, Dresden, Germany. The molar conductivity measurements of freshly prepared solutions (c = 1 mmol L−1) were performed on a Jenway 4510 conductivity meter, Dunmow, UK. 1H and 13C/13C{1H} NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance III spectrometer (Bruker, Rheinstetten, Germany), operating at 400 and 100.6 MHz, respectively. Two-dimensional experiments (gradient 1H–1H COSY, HSQC and HMBC and NOESY) were run on the same instrument utilizing the built-in Bruker pulse sequences. All NMR spectra were measured at 25 ◦C in perdeuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d6) with tetramethylsilane (δH 0, δC 0) as the internal reference standard. Coupling constants were expressed in Hz.

## Computational Details

All calculations used molecular structures extracted from crystal structure models (our model for triclinic polymorph 3a and CSD refcode GIMBUZ02, deposition number 1429891 [23], for monoclinic polymorph 3b). Prior to calculations, X–H bond lengths were normalized to neutron diffraction values [24].
Pairwise intermolecular interaction energies were calculated for the first coordination shell of the molecules in both polymorphs, and their energy was partitioned into electrostatic, polarization, dispersion, and repulsion terms using generalized Kohn–Sham energy decomposition analysis [25]:
∆Etot = ∆Eele + ∆Epol + ∆Edisp + ∆Erep (1)
The calculation employed the M06-2X functional [26] and the def2-TZVP basis set [27] as implemented in XEDA (ver. 0.0.1) [28]. Interaction energies were corrected for the basis set superposition error by the counter-poise method [29].
The pairwise interaction energies were represented as vectors, originating from the geometric center of the reference molecule M0 and directed toward a neighboring interacting molecule Mi. where Ri is the distance between the geometric centers of the interacting molecules M0 and Mi, Ei is the interaction energy for each molecular pair (∆Etot from Equation (1)), and Estr is the energy of the strongest interaction within the crystal [30–32]. Energy vector diagrams were visualized in VESTA (ver. 3.5.7) [33].
Crystal lattice energies were computed by the summation of pairwise terms for the cluster of molecules surrounding the central molecule using CE-B3LYP model energies [34] with CrystalExplorer21 (ver. 21.5) [35], where TONTO (ver. 21.03.16) was used as the backend [36]. The cluster included all molecules with an atom–atom distance to the central molecule less than 30 Å to ensure convergence. CrystalExplorer21 was used for Hirshfeld surface analysis.

## Results and Discussion

3.1. Synthesis and Characterization
The single crystals of the ligand [HL]NO3 (1a) were obtained, as reported earlier [7]. During the examination of its coordination affinities towards zinc and organotin (Figure 1), the crystallization of two ligand polymorphs occurred, i.e., the known one (1a) and a new one (1b). Namely, the reaction of 1a and zinc acetate, as well as zinc iodide, yielded single crystals of that known ligand polymorph. On the contrary, the reaction with zinc bromide and dimethyltin dichloride furnished single crystals of 1b, and when zinc chloride was used, 1a appeared to be the first fraction, while 1b was obtained as the second fraction.

Even though the complexes were not obtained, these results are significant since they enable the comparative structural analysis of those compounds and similar ones, thus creating a better explanation of the structure–property relation. This is not unusual since it was reported that several other Schiff bases of aminoguanidine also crystallized during the attempts to obtain complexes [8,37]. The identification of the species influencing the mode of crystallization merits further research.
Attempts to neutralize 1a using a strong base such as KOH facilitated the formation of a cocrystal consisting of a neutral molecule, the nitrate salt of the Schiff base, and a molecule of water, [HL]NO3·L·H2O (2). The partial neutralization of the starting compound was not surprising since the alkaline character of the aminoguanidine moiety is well known [11]. The research has shown that even acylaminoguanidines act as relatively strong bases even though their carbonyl group has electron-withdrawing properties [38].
Finally, the possibility of in situ condensation of the free amino group of the aminoguanidine residue was examined, similar to [38]. Thus, what was planned to be a template reaction, i.e., the reaction of ammonium metavanadate with 1a in the presence of the excess of salicylaldehyde, unexpectedly gave a purely organic product (3), a diimine of the salicylaldehyde aminal (Figure 2).
Figure 2. The reaction scheme and structural formula of unexpected product 3.
Even though two polymorphs of compound 3, one triclinic 3a and one monoclinic 3b, have been known for a long time, the data on those synthetic procedures are relatively poor [39]. However, the compounds containing CN3 core are essential due to their versatile coordination mode and the possibility of building numerous oligomers and polymers. Thus, several accessible synthetic routes for guanidine-based derivatives have been suggested [40]. Guanidinium moieties were identified as promising for incorporation into porous ionic covalent organic nanosheets for use as antimicrobial agents, where the positive charge of these species is of paramount interest [41]. Hence, it is still worthwhile to learn more about the synthesis and structures of these compounds and their complexes.
The reported synthesis of 3 was attempted as a means to obtain a vanadium (V) complex with a tetradentate ligand, where vanadium(V) would act as a template for additional condensation of the free NH2-group of the Schiff base and salicylaldehyde molecule. Reactions of this kind were reported earlier, i.e., in the reaction of salicylideneaminoguanidine and salicylaldehyde in an alkaline solution in the presence of Ni(II) and VO(IV), the complexes with tetradentate ligand were obtained. However, these complexes were not structurally characterized [5]. Even though the reaction described here had an unexpected course, it could easily be explained by the mechanism shown in Figure 3, which suggests two pathways to the diimine product. Vanadate does not seem to play a crucial role in the reaction course. Once formed by the action of ammonia, a cationic aminal species can either be transformed by the loss of ammonia into a reactive intermediate (path A) or simply react with a salicylaldehyde molecule (path B).

All obtained compounds were isolated as single crystals stable in air and suitable for the XRD experiments. Compounds 1 and 2 were characterized by elemental analysis and IR and NMR spectroscopy, while the data for 3a were in concordance with the previously reported [39].
Comparing the IR spectra of 1 and 2, many similarities are revealed. Bands at 1690 and 1683 cm−1 could be ascribed to C=N vibrations, while the bands originating from C–O and guanido group vibrations appear at ≈1265 and ≈1630 cm−1. Additionally, in these spectra, a band at 1385 cm−1 results from NO3−ion vibrations [7]. The bands in the region of 3400–3200 cm−1 correspond to NH2- and NH-groups.
The assignment of all signals in NMR spectra of 1–3 was made possible by 2D NMR techniques, as well as 13C proton-coupled NMR experiments (Figures S3–S24). 1H and 13C{1H} NMR spectra of 1 and 2 appear quite similar, with the main dissimilarities observed in chemical shift and, due to the hydrate nature of 2, the shape and integral of the residual water signal in 1H NMR. Differences in the 1H and 13C shifts of the two compounds can be attributed to the fact that rapid proton transfer occurs between the protonated and unprotonated species of L existing in the dimethyl sulfoxide solution of 2; the consequence of this is an averaging of the chemical shifts of the nuclei of the two species to produce resonances found in the spectra of 2. A sharp singlet in the 1H NMR of 1 arising from the phenol-group proton clearly indicated the existence of an intramolecular hydrogen bond. The exact same type of hydrogen bonding also shifted signals of two equivalent phenolgroup protons of 3 downfield with regard to the remaining phenol group incapable of intramolecular hydrogen bonding, as later confirmed by crystallography (cf. Section 3.4). It was also found that 3 gradually hydrolyzed in contact with residual moisture (Figure S24).

The asymmetric unit of 1b consists of the salicylidene-aminoguanidinium ion HL+
with a protonated aminoguanidinium fragment and a single nitrate ion. The structural parameters of this cation (Figure 4) are in concordance with those found in the structure of 1a [7], as well as the other structures containing the same moiety ([HL]Cl·H2O·½EtOH, [HL][CuCl3(H2O)], [HL]2[Cu(H2O)6](SO4)2·2H2O [42,43]).

Due to the wide delocalization of electron density in aminoguanidinium fragment bonds, C1–N1, C1–N2 and C1–N4 are shorter than single and longer than double bonds (Table 1). This makes the cation highly planar (with an average deviation from the mean plane of 0.05 Å). The conformation of the cation is suitable for coordination due to the strong intramolecular O1–H1···N3 bond. Apart from this, aminoguanidinium cations and nitrate ions form the H-bond network, with all of the H-donors and H-acceptors involved. This leads to the stabilization of the crystal structure of 1b (Figure 5), in which cationic (x = 1/2) and anionic layers (x = 1/4) are organized alternately.

3.3. Crystal Structure of 2
The attempts to neutralize 1a lead to the formation of a cocrystal 2 in which protonated and neutral forms of Schiff base coexist. Thus, the asymmetric unit of 2 contains HL+, L, a nitrate ion and one water molecule (Figure 6). As in 1b, intramolecular H-bonds in both HL+ and L enable the conformation with all possible ligators of the same orientation.
Figure 6. The asymmetric unit of 2 and H-bonds therein.
This is the first structure in which the free neutral form of this Schiff base is obtained, which allows the comparative analysis of the geometry features of the cation and the molecule. In 2, all bond lengths in HL+ are similar to those in all the other structures with the same moiety. However, those differ from the parameters found in the structure of L (Table 1). One of the main differences is the deprotonation of nitrogen atom N6, which leads to the shortening of the C9–N6 bond and the elongation of the C9–N5 and C9–N8 bonds. The values of torsion angles (τ(N3–N2–C1–N4) = −171.5(1)◦; τ(N7–N6–C9–N8) = 172.9(1)◦) suggest twisting around N2–C1 (HL+) and N6–C9 (L) bonds, causing higher deviation from the mean planes for terminal nitrogen atoms compared with the same in 1b (0.187 for N1, 0.153 for N4, 0.181 for N5 and 0.259 Å for N8). In L, the additional torsion occurs around the C17–N7 bond (τ(C15–C17–N7–N6) = 175.0(1)◦). The crystal packing of structural units of 2 is given in Figure 7.

3.4. Crystal Structures of the Polymorphs 3a and 3b—Insights from an Energetic Perspective
A previously published paper [39] discussed only geometrical differences in the crystal structures of the two polymorphs and suggested that the higher melting point of the monoclinic polymorph may be caused by differences in hydrogen bonding geometry, i.e., a shortened hydrogen bond. However, contemporary tools allow for the reinvestigation of crystal packing from an energetic point of view by calculating the interaction energies of molecular pairs within the crystal structure [32]. This approach provides additional insights into structural differences and ranks intermolecular interactions by their contribution to the total interaction energy [30,44].

3.4.2. Crystal Structure of the Monoclinic Polymorph 3b
The building unit of a crystal structure is a single molecule, encompassed by 14 neighboring molecules in its first coordination sphere. Among these, four neighboring molecules are particularly notable due to their significant intermolecular interaction energies. Two of these molecules are hydrogen-bonded to the central molecule, each exhibiting interaction energies of ∆E = −35.5 kJ mol−1, representing the strongest interactions within the crystal structure (Figure 10a). The two hydrogen-bonded molecules are related to the central molecule through a two-fold screw axis parallel to the crystallographic axis b. Geometrical parameters of this hydrogen bond are d(O1–H1) = 0.82 Å, d(H1···O2) = 1.86 Å, d(O1···O2) = 2.6797(18) Å and α(O1–H1···O2) = 176.7◦.
The interaction in polymorph 3b is slightly less stabilizing compared to the analogous interaction (Table 3) in polymorph 3a, exhibiting energy more in line with values commonly reported in the literature [45,46]. In polymorph 3b, there is a greater contribution from the electrostatic and polarization terms, consistent with the shorter hydrogen bond observed in this polymorph compared to 3a. However, dispersion forces in 3b are weaker, and repulsive interactions are more pronounced, leading to a lower overall interaction energy. This variation in interaction energy can be ascribed to the differences in the spatial arrangement of hydrogen-bonded dimers, which are related by translation in 3a and by a two-fold screw axis in 3b.

3.4.4. Estimation of Crystal Lattice Energies of 3a and 3b
Calculating crystal lattice energies is crucial for comparing the stability of polymorphs, as it provides insight into the relative thermodynamic favorability of different crystal structures. The CrystalExplorer CE-B3LYP model, which employs intermolecular interaction energies derived from quantum mechanical calculations, is a widely used tool for such analyses. To internally validate the accuracy of CE-B3LYP model energies, a comparison was performed with intermolecular energies calculated using XEDA (Figure S1, Tables S2 and S3). Although CE-B3LYP tends to overestimate individual binding energies by approximately 20% compared to M06-2X/def2-TZVP, it nonetheless shows an excellent correlation (Pearson’s R = 0.997). Given that we are primarily concerned with relative polymorph stability, this overestimation should not affect the overall conclusions.
Conformational flexibility can significantly influence the results of the computational estimation of relative polymorph stability through the calculation of lattice energies (Figure S2) [48]. However, it has been shown that in the case of rigid molecules, such as 1,4diiodobenzene and 5-fluorouracil, meaningful estimates can be obtained by the application of the CE-B3LYP model [49]. Minor conformational differences between molecules in the two polymorphic crystal structures, 3a and 3b, under investigation should allow the direct assessment of crystal lattice energies without the need for conformational energy correction.
The obtained values are, as expected for polymorphs of molecular solids [50], quite similar. The crystal lattice energy of the triclinic polymorph 3a was computed to be −179.1 kJ mol−1, while that of the monoclinic polymorph 3b was calculated to be −174.7 kJ mol−1. These results are not in line with the higher melting point of the monoclinic polymorph 3b (436 K) compared to the triclinic polymorph 3a (416 K) [39]. Furthermore, the calculated crystal packing index and density indicate that polymorph 3b is packed more efficiently (Table S4), indicating greater stability.

## Conclusions

The attempts to neutralize and examine the coordination behavior of the Schiff base of aminoguanidine and salicylaldehyde gave unexpected results. Namely, in the reaction of the Schiff base nitrate salt with zinc salts and organotin compounds, one known and one new polymorph of the ligand were formed, while the reaction with potassium hydroxide yielded a cocrystal in which partial neutralization of the Schiff base occurred. The reaction of ammonium metavanadate resulted in the formation of a diimine product. All novel compounds were characterized by SC-XRD, and a detailed comparative analysis of their structure was conducted. The structural features of the Schiff base cation present in 1b and 2 are in concordance with the previously reported. However, due to the deprotonation of one nitrogen atom, differences are observed in the neutral form of the Schiff base present in 2.
In both polymorphs, 3a and 3b, the building unit is a single molecule. The strongest interactions are mediated by an O–H···O hydrogen bond, which connects the building units into columns. The energetic hierarchy and directionality of other interactions are profoundly different in the two polymorphs. This leads to a columnar-layered structure type for the triclinic polymorph 3a and an isotropic structure type for the monoclinic polymorph 3b. Calculations of crystal lattice energies for both polymorphs show that those are very similar and indicate that the triclinic polymorph is more stable. These features are not reflected in the values of calculated crystal densities and experimentally reported melting points.
