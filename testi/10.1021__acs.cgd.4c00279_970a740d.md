# Unraveling a Unique Metastable Phase of a Proxyphylline Cocrystal Exclusively Produced by Compressed Carbon Dioxide

DOI: 10.1021/acs.cgd.4c00279

## Abstract

In this work, racemic proxyphylline ((±)-PXL, a chiral drug) was cocrystallized for the first time with 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (4-HBA) from three solvents (acetone, ethyl acetate, and isobutanol) and using CO 2 as an antisolvent. The obtained solid forms were compared to those produced by liquid-assisted grinding (LAG). It is shown that though both methods produce a racemic compound (±)-PXL 1:4-HBA 1 cocrystal, LAG promotes the crystallization of an orthorhombic P na2 1 form (Phase I ), whereas CO 2 -assisted crystallization gives rise to an original monoclinic C 2/c form with partial inclusion of CO 2 and water molecules (Phase II ). The structure of Phase II originates from this inclusion since this phase is never obtained in the absence of CO 2 . Concomitant cocrystallization of Phase I and Phase II occurs during CO 2 -assisted crystallization in proportions that vary with solvents and temperature. Our study shows that Phase II transforms progressively into Phase I upon storage or heating.

## Introduction

Owing to the emergence of green chemistry, supercritical CO2 (scCO2) has been proposed for
material syntheses in partial or total replacement of the conventional volatile organic compounds
(VOCs) solvents. The use of scCO2 for particle micronization is widely investigated since it allows
to overcome some of the inherent limitations of conventional mechanical treatment, chemical
precipitation or spray-drying1. The special feature of supercritical CO2 as a crystallization medium
is that it allows the solvation ability to be controlled by varying the operating temperature and
pressure, further influencing the characteristics of produced particles2–5.
The application of scCO2 to crystal engineering and especially to cocrystal formation has
emerged more recently6–11. Most of these supercritical methods involve CO2 as an antisolvent,
being the GAS (Gaseous AntiSolvent) or the variants that employ solution spraying like SAS
(Supercritical AntiSolvent), SEDS (Solution Enhanced Dispersion by Supercritical fluids) or
2
ASES (Aerosol Solvent Extraction System)1. CO2 can also be used as a solvent leading to a
completely dry route known as CSS (Cocrystallization with Supercritical Solvent)7,12. Cocrystals
are solid state multicomponent assemblies made of a given molecule and one or several coformers
that are held together by weak interactions. A consolidated account of the rationale for design of
cocrystals was provided by Sathisaran in 201813, so the general framework involved in successful
(or unsuccessful) cocrystallization is not detailed here. It should be mentioned, however, that while
molecular interactions between components are necessary, thermodynamics and kinetics also
control cocrystal formation14–16. Cocrystals are classically produced by mechanochemical methods
(neat or liquid-assisted grinding, LAG), solvent-evaporation or cooling, or even reactive
crystallization in suitable solvents, though each of these methods present different issues. The
production of a chemically and structurally pure cocrystal powder remains challenging specially
for a process route based on the addition of an antisolvent which involves at least four different
species17–19.
Beyond the fact that cocrystals are solid forms different from the solid reactants they originate,
literature also lately reported several examples of polymorphic cocrystals20–23 and solvate
cocrystals24–27. These are mostly produced by conventional methods. Though the potentiality of
scCO2-based techniques to control polymorphism has been addressed for homocrystals28–37, only
few articles reported polymorphism of cocrystals38,39, one the production of a solvate28, and none
has ever reported the obtention of a cocrystal solvate. Recently, when investigating the
polymorphism of Praziquantel, De Moraes et al. accessed the hydrate40 found through supercritical
CO2 processing by MacEachern28. Contrarily, the new metastable form of deoxycholic acid (the
authors called a polymorph) achieved by contacting the powder with supercritical carbon dioxide41
has never been reported since. To our best knowledge, the ability of scCO2 to produce an original
3
solid form, that cannot be achieved by conventional routes, has not been reported yet for a
cocrystal. It is worth to note that screening all the possible crystal forms is very often mandatory
before considering any application for pharmaceutical companies. The preliminary knowledge of
these forms, especially the unexpected ones resulting from non-conventional processes is then of
great interest for the scientific community.
Proxyphylline (PXL) is a chiral theophylline derivative (Scheme 1a) that presents a rich solid-
state landscape42: there are two and three polymorphic forms for the racemic compounds and for
the  enantiopure crystals, respectively. A cocrystal screening of PXL with several achiral
coformers was performed by Harfouche et al.43 using solvent crystallization, neat grinding and
LAG. Several new solid forms were reported, among which a conglomerate co-crystal of PXL
with 2-hydroxy benzoic acid and water, that was resolved by preferential crystallization44. Besides,
PXL crystallization or cocrystallization by CO2-based techniques has never been reported. In the
present publication, we report the successful co-crystallization of racemic PXL ((±)-PXL
hereafter) with 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (4-HBA hereafter) (Scheme 1b) by scCO2 process.  By this
way, it was possible to isolate a new solid form that can only be achieved by scCO2 crystallization
processes; the crystal structure determination highlights a very rare case of incorporation of CO2
as guest molecule inside the crystal lattice.

## Experimental section

Materials and solvents. Racemic proxyphylline, (±)-3,7-dihydro-7-(2-hydroxypropyl)-1,3-
dimethyl-1H-purine-2,6-dione, 98%, (±)-PXL) and 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (99%, 4-HBA) were
supplied from TCI EUROPE (Belgium). Carbon dioxide (CO2, 99.5%) was supplied from Messer
(France). Acetone (HPLC grade), isobutanol, absolute ethanol and ethyl acetate (analytical grade)
were purchased from Atlantic Labo (France).
Solubility measurements. Solubilities of (±)-PXL and 4-HBA were estimated by the solid
addition method.  A known amount of powder was added to a given volume of solvent and stirred
at 500 rpm and 25°C for 2 hours. Additions were iterated until a persistent cloudy solution was
obtained. The tests were performed for commercial racemic PXL (RI phase) and 4-HBA in ethanol,
acetone, isobutanol, and ethyl acetate.
Cocrystal preparation
By Liquid Assisted Grinding (LAG)
Liquid assisted grinding (LAG) was carried out at room temperature using a Retsch Vibratory
Mill MM400 working with two 10 mL zirconia jars containing one 12 mm diameter zirconia ball.
Grinding was performed at a frequency of 30 Hz for 30 minutes and experiments involved a 1:1
molar ratio between (±)-PXL and 4-HBA. 20 μL of either acetone, ethanol, ethyl acetate and
isobutanol were then added (solvent:powder molar ratio: 1:3).
Assisted by compressed CO2 - GAS method
Most cocrystallization trials were carried out using the GAS (Gaseous AntiSolvent) method,
which essentially consists of gradually adding CO2 to a solution containing the species to be
precipitated. The apparatus is described elsewhere6. Compressed CO2 was first cooled in a cryostat
5
at 0°C and introduced by a high-pressure pump (EM1, Lewa, Germany) into a 0.49 L stainless
steel vessel equipped with three sapphire windows, and a filter at its bottom. The vessel was heated
at 35°C before the introduction of 30 mL of the [(±)-PXL + 4-HBA + solvent] solution. The
solution was previously pre-heated at 35°C on a hot plate. For each solvent, the concentration of
(±)-PXL was set to 0.9 fold its solubility value at 25°C and the concentration of 4-HBA was
adjusted accordingly to fit a (±)-PXL:4-HBA molar ratio of 1:1. In this GAS set-up, the CO2
dispersed directly into the solution through the blades of a Rushton turbine whose rotation was set
at 300 rpm until reaching the desired pressure of 10 MPa. During this step, as CO2 was added
gradually, the pressure inside the vessel increased and the solvent-CO2 mixture shifted from a
liquid-vapor system to a monophasic fluid. At the same time, the solubility of the components in
this solvent-CO2 mixture decreased until the solubility limit was reached, resulting in a
precipitation event that could be observed through the sapphire windows. After the pressurization
step, the bulk fluid was evacuated from the bottom of the reactor while feeding it with CO2 at 25
g/min for 90 minutes at 500 rpm to maintain the pressure and to dry the produced powder. For
some experiments, a static step was maintained for 1 hour at a constant pressure of 10 MPa before
flushing out the bulk fluid. Finally, the vessel was depressurized, and the powder was collected,
weighed, and characterized.  Precipitation yield was calculated from the mass of initial mixture

Powder characterization
X-Ray Diffractometry: powder and single crystal
Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns of GAS and SEDS samples were collected by using
a PANalitycal X’pert PRO MPD and a Xcelerator Philips diffractometer, respectively, in Bragg-
Brentano θ-θ geometry equipped with a secondary monochromator and X’Celerator multi-strip
detector. The GAS sample patterns were recorded in the angular range of 4-38° 2θ for 113 min,
whereas for SEDS samples, the angular range was 2-40° 2θ and lasted 15 min. The Cu-Kα
radiation was generated at 45 KV and 40 mA (l = 0.15406 nm). The samples were gently ground
to limit preferred orientations. The powders were then placed uniformly on sample holders made
of aluminum alloy and flattened with a razor blade. Single crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD)
was performed with Mo Kα (λ = 0.71073 Å) radiation on a Bruker Kappa APEXII, using graphite
7
monochromator and an Oxford cryosystems 700 N2 cryostream. Data were collected and reduced
using the software included in the Bruker Apex4 suite45. The structures were solved using direct
methods or intrinsic phasing. Non-hydrogen atoms were refined anisotropically using weighted
full-matrix least-squares on F2, followed by difference Fourier synthesis using the SHELX
programs suite46. All hydrogen atoms were included in the final structure factor calculation at
idealized positions and were allowed to ride on the neighboring atoms with relative isotropic
displacement coefficients.
Thermogravimetric and Mass Spectrometry analysis (TGA-MS)
A thermogravimetric analysis coupled with mass spectrometry was performed using a TGA
Setaram TGA2400 combined with a Pfeiffer Thermostar mass spectrometer. The measurements
were carried out in argon atmosphere in the range 20 – 140°C using a heating rate of 10 °C/min.
Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)
Thermal properties of CO2-processed cocrystals were analyzed using the Mettler Toledo DSC2
system equipped with the HSS8 (High Sensitivity Sensor) sensor coupled with a LF1100 Mettler
Toledo thermogravimetric analyzer. Samples of around 10 mg were heated in a pierced aluminum
crucible at 10°C/min from -25°C to 200°C under 50 mL/min N2 purge.
Second Harmonic Generation Spectroscopy
A Nd:YAG Q-switched laser (Quantel) operating at 1.06 µm was used to deliver up to 360 mJ
pulses of 5 ns duration with a repetition rate of 10 Hz. An energy adjustment device made up of
two polarizers (P) and a half-wave plate (k/2) allowed the incident energy to vary from 0 to ca.
200 mJ per pulse. A RG1000 filter was used after the energy adjustment device to remove light
from laser flash lamps. The samples (few mg of powder in a crucible) were placed in a computer-
8
controlled Heating-Cooling stage (Linkam THMS-600) and were irradiated with a beam 4 mm in
diameter.
Raman Spectroscopy
A Raman spectrometer was used to characterize SEDS samples (RamanRxn2, Kaiser Optical
Systems, with a spectral resolution of 4 cm-1 mean at 785 nm). The probe is Rxn2Single-785,
number of acquisitions: 2028 with an exposure time of 5 seconds.
High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC)
The composition in 4-HBA, R-PXL and S-PXL of the produced powders was determined by
chiral chromatography. Aliquots of powders were first dissolved in acetone (concentration of 1
mg/mL) and filtrated through 0.2 µm syringe filters. HPLC analysis was performed by an Agilent
Infinity 1200 system (Agilent Technologies) equipped with a degasser, a quaternary pump, an
automatic injector, a diode array detector (DAD, G1315A) and a Chemstation software for data
acquisition. The column was a Chiralpak IC (250 x 4.6 mm, 5μm particle size, Daicel Chiral
Technologies Europe, Illkirch, France). The temperature was set at 25°C. The mobile phase,
flowing at 1.0 mL/min, consisted of a mixture of 75% heptane and 25% ethanol (v/v). The
separation was achieved in 30 min in isocratic mode. The used wavelength for UV detection was
273 nm. The calibration curves ranged from 125 and 1000 µg/mL for both 4-HBA and (±) -PXL
(thus from 62.5 and 500 µg/mL for each PXL enantiomer), assuming the same response factor for
each enantiomer. Analyses were performed in triplicate.

## Results and discussion

Solvent selection: VLE and solubility screening
Crystallization solvents were mainly chosen considering their eligibility with the gaseous
antisolvent (GAS, hereafter) or Solution Enhanced Dispersion with Supercritical Fluids (SEDS
hereafter) recrystallization processes (toxicity and miscibility with CO2). In a solid form screening
context, several solvents have to be tested to maximize the chance of producing a new phase. It is
also well established that different solvents may give rises to different polymorphs.30,34,37,47
Acetone, ethyl acetate, ethanol and isobutanol were therefore investigated.
The vapor-liquid equilibrium (VLE) of the CO2–solvent mixtures at 40°C (above the critical
temperature of CO2) are represented in Figure 148–50. At this temperature and for all solvents, the
critical pressure of the mixture is about 8 MPa. According to the VLE, the solubility of CO2 in the
liquid phase is much larger in ethyl acetate and acetone than in ethanol or isobutanol. For several
compounds precipitated by GAS from acetone solution, the precipitation occurred around 6-7
MPa, which is lower than the critical pressure of the mixture9,13. A pressure of 6 MPa corresponds
to a minimum CO2 composition of 80% mol in the liquid phase. In the case of alcohols, it is
necessary to reach nearly 8 MPa to obtain such CO2 composition in the liquid phase, while for ethyl
acetate, a pressure of 4.3 MPa is sufficient.

A preliminary test of cocrystallization of (±)-PXL and salicylic acid (SA) by GAS failed, in that
only PXL homocrystals were produced. As a result, 4-HBA was chosen as the coformer due to its
solubility 2 orders of magnitude lower in CO2 than that of SA51.
Table 1 summarizes the solvent critical properties and the saturation concentration of racemic
proxyphylline and 4-hydroxybenzoic acid in the four organic solvents. The solubility of 4-HBA is
much higher than that of (±)-PXL in all solvents. Its lowest value is at 11.4 mmol/mol in ethyl
acetate, while (±)-PXL shows its highest solubility at 6.1 mmol/mol in ethanol. It is worth noting
that high solubility in a solvent has a detrimental effect on the precipitation induced by pressurized
CO2 as antisolvent52–54. The solubility ratios of 4-HBA to (±)-PXL range from 8 to 14, with the
smallest ratio corresponding to ethyl acetate. In the specific case of GAS experiments, ethanol was
not considered since unsuccessful precipitations were already reported 14,16.

Crystal structure determination of new phases
Some attempts were made to prepare single crystals suitable for diffraction experiments by slow
evaporation of (±)-PXL:4-HBA equimolar saturated solutions made with various solvents. We
succeeded in isolating a single crystal for Phase I but, in agreement with previous observation, it
14
was not possible to grow any Phase II crystal with this method.  Nevertheless, a single crystal
suitable for X-ray diffraction could be collected from a CO2-processed powder and was confirmed
later to match with the Phase II PXRD. Single crystal X-ray diffraction analyses were thus
performed on both samples.
Crystal Structure of Phase I
The colorless plate-like crystal of Phase I (0.025×0.2×0.25 mm3) has been mounted onto a
microloop with Paraton oil under a N2 cryostream at 150K. Phase I crystallizes in the orthorhombic
Pna21 non-centrosymmetric space group with the following unit cell parameters: a=30.439(5) Å,
b=7.6469(14) Å, c=7.4413(13) Å and V=1732.1(5) Å3. All the crystallographic information and
refinement quality factors are gathered in Supplementary Information as Table S1. The asymmetric
unit is constituted of one molecule of PXL and one molecule of 4-HBA leading to a (±)-
PXL:4HBA cocrystal with 1:1 stoichiometry (Figure 3).
Figure 3. View of the asymmetric unit of Phase I, confirming the formation of the 1:1 (±)-
PXL:4-HBA cocrystal. Thermal displacement ellipsoids are drawn at the 50% level.
Excluding the propanol moiety of PXL, both molecules are very planar (Root Mean Square
Deviation (RMSD) = 0.058 Å, max deviation = 0.134 for C8 for PXL; RMSD = 0.006 Å, max

leading to a “zig zag” 3D arrangement within the crystal lattice (Supplementary Information Figure

all considered as moderate to strong according to the Jeffrey classification55,56 leading to a
relatively strongly cohesive network. These results confirm that Phase I is an asolvated (±)-PXL1:4-
HBA1 racemic compound co-crystal.
Crystal Structure of Phase II
The tiny colorless crystal of Phase II (0.02×0.02×0.1 mm3) was mounted onto a microloop with
Paraton oil at 120K. Phase II crystallizes in the monoclinic C2/c centrosymmetric space group
with the following unit cell parameters: a=22.274(2) Å, b=11.843(1) Å, c=14.439(1) Å,
β=97.326(3)°, V=3777.8(6) Å3. Crystallographic information and refinement quality factors are
gathered in Table S1. The asymmetric unit is constituted of one molecule of PXL and one molecule
of 4-HBA, which confirms the racemic compound nature of this phase and the (±)-PXL1:4-HBA1
stoichiometry. Moreover, additional strongly disordered light atoms which were difficult to
identify have been also detected in the asymmetric unit: They have been assigned to oxygen with
partial occupation factors for refinement but could correspond to any small molecule compatible
with the ca. 80 Å3 of the cavities (2 water molecules approximately or other molecules with a
16
similar volume). The propanol moiety of the PXL molecule is also strongly disordered especially
at the -OH function, which is very probably due to interactions with solvent molecules standing in
statistical different positions. The interactions of the solvent molecules with the rest of the crystal
structure should thus be relatively weak. When the squeeze procedure57,58 was applied to refine
the crystal structure excluding the disordered solvent, 76 Å3 cavities corresponding to ca. 25
electrons were found. Both values are compatible with 2 water molecules or one other molecule
of similar volume like methanol or CO2. Other solvents used in the crystallization processes seem
to be too large to fit with the cavities size (Figure 5).

Recrystallizations by CO2-SEDS
Co-crystallization of (±)-PXL and 4-HBA was then attempted using the SEDS version of CO2
antisolvent. Unlike the GAS method, SEDS operates by spraying the solution inside pressurized
CO2, giving rise to a monophasic supersaturated phase in conditions of turbulent flow. It provides
an immediate high content of CO2 at the vicinity of the nozzle tip (xCO2 ≈1) and crystallization is
almost instantaneous. Pressure and temperature conditions for the test performed in acetone were
12 MPa and 50°C respectively. Figure 11 shows the PXRD patterns of (±)-PXL:4-HBA powder
obtained by this method in comparison to that obtained by the GAS method working with
isobutanol.

Figure 11 confirms that pure Phase II is also obtained by the SEDS methods since no trace of
Phase I was detected. Therefore, in acetone, pure Phase II was not achieved by GAS but it was
reached by SEDS, confirming the mandatory high CO2 fraction to achieve this result. When the
PXL:4HBA ratio was changed for 1:2 or 1:0.5 instead of 1:1, extra peaks appeared in the
diffractograms according to the component in excess, but Phase II was still the only produced

the obtained powder was a mixture of Phases I and II, highlighting the fact that mixing conditions
influence the produced cocrystal form, as for single compound crystallization30,63. The trials by
SEDS, that was performed by another team, did confirm the fabrication of the new specific Phase
II for PXL:4-HBA cocrystal when processed by CO2 antisolvent.
The overall results obtained by GAS and SEDS confirmed that formation of Phase II requires a
very CO2-rich environment. In GAS, it forms when the fluid system is at the vicinity of the critical
point and the bubble points line and during the continuation of the process, conditions continue to
evolve (addition of CO2 up to 10 MPa, filtration of the suspension and drying under a compressed
CO2 flux) so Phase I can nucleate or more likely Phase II evolves to Phase I according to powder
ageing discussed later, yielding to bi-latticed populations. Extra experiments are required to
specifically investigate the control of Phase II content by acting for instance on kinetics, but so far
it could be assumed that Phase II is a metastable form that appears earlier than Phase I in the
process.
According to the Rietveld refinements, it was found that the same solvated cocrystal (Phase II)
was always obtained by CO2 techniques regardless of the solvent and of the CO2-process used (i.e.,
29
GAS or SEDS). As the only common molecule was the antisolvent, CO2 was very likely to be the
molecules inserted in Phase II. Nevertheless, one could not exclude that the organics solvents used,
or H2O present in them, or even more likely atmospheric H2O, could insert into the crystal structure
during the process or upon storage before XRD analysis. Thermogravimetric measurements
coupled with mass spectrometry were therefore carried out to elucidate the nature of the guest
component.
Transformations of solid phases
Thermogravimetric measurements
The weight and the associated compounds loss responses of CO2-processed cocrystals within the
temperature range 25-130°C were investigated by thermogravimetric analyses coupled with mass
spectrometry (TGA-MS). Two samples were studied by TGA-MS, both containing Phase II but
obtained from two solvents: isobutanol (pure Phase II, Figure 12a) and acetone (Figure 12b). Since
the acetone sample was a mixture of phases rich in Phase II, it was checked by TGA that Phase I
did not show any mass loss under heating (Figure S5).
As shown by TGA on Figure 12, both samples exhibited a mass loss of 3.4% when temperature
increased from 40 to 110°C; a unique mass loss event was detected for the powder obtained from
isobutanol, while a two-step behavior was clearly observed for the acetone processed sample with
a first loss of 1.1% between 40 and 80°C and a much more abrupt mass loss above 80°C. According
to the MS results, these two steps were correlated to: (i) the removal of water before 80 °C and (ii)
to the removal of significant amount of CO2 and of a smaller amount of water and acetone when
temperature increased above 80°C (Figure 12b). For the isobutanol processed sample, a much
smaller amount of CO2 was released around the same temperature, suggesting that CO2 occupied
30
the same sites but in much lower quantities (no trace of isobutanol was detected). It is also worth
noting that, in both cases, the first mass loss occurs on a wide temperature range whereas the
second mass loss occurs on a narrower temperature range. This suggests that two types of
crystallographic sites for the inclusion components exist in this structure: the first one seems
playing an important role in the scaffold of the Phase II structure and is occupied by water
molecules, the second site can be occupied by other solvent molecules. Their departure will
certainly lead to a collapse of the structure with a return back to the asolvated Phase I.  In all
samples tested, CO2 is the last molecule extracted from the structure confirming that its presence
seems to be crucial for the existence of Phase II.

While the inclusion component was essentially water for the isobutanol processed sample, the
acetone processed sample contained significant quantities of CO2. Depending hence of the solvent
used for the cocrystallization process, different amounts of CO2 were included into the crystal onto
specific sites leading to different ratio of CO2/H2O in the cocrystal structure. The higher solubility
of CO2 in acetone than in isobutanol (Figure 1) may explain the higher ratio of inserted CO2 for the
acetone processed sample. According to these results, a possible chemical composition for the
phase-II would be (C10H14N4O3)1(C7H6O3)1.xH2O,yCO2 (with x and y < 1).
So far, it is the first example of inclusion of CO2 in a cocrystal. It is also remarkable that the
structure of Phase II originates from this inclusion of CO2 molecules since this phase has never
been obtained in the absence of CO2.

## Conclusion

Co-crystallization of (±)-PXL with 4-HBA by using the conventional dry or liquid assisted
grinding (LAG) or the CO2-antisolvent techniques (GAS, SEDS) systematically led to the
crystallization of a new cocrystal with formula (±)-PXL1:4-HBA1. This co-crystal exhibits two solid
phases, Phase I and II whose crystallization depends on the technique used for the cocrystal
production. Phase I is the stable form under atmospheric conditions and can be easily prepared by
LAG but also occurs during crystallization with CO2 based techniques. In contrast, Phase II can
only be produced by CO2 techniques. It has a layered structure allowing the presence of cavities
inside which small solvent molecules can intercalate. Thermogravimetric analysis coupled with
mass spectrometry revealed that CO2 and H2O in significant quantities (3.4 wt %) were trapped in
the Phase II structure, at a composition that depends on the solvent used as initial liquor. When
isobutanol is used, the inclusion is essentially made up of water with a small amount of CO2,
whereas in acetone, there is almost as much water as CO2.
The CO2-operating conditions, such as the choice of solvent and temperature in GAS, were found
to determine the purity of Phase II in the produced powders. To favour the production of pure
Phase II cocrystals, trials can be carried out in GAS from isobutanol at 40°C. At this stage of
investigations, it was suggested that richness in Phase II could be related to the proximity of the
thermodynamic conditions of precipitation to the critical point of the mixture and the line of bubble
points and/or to time given to Phase II to evolve towards Phase I, but this needs to be confirmed
by future works. So far, experiments carried out at 50°C using the higher composition in CO2 and
more intense mixing provided by the SEDS technique, did confirm the fabrication of (±)-PXL1:4-
HBA1 cocrystal as a pure Phase II even in acetone.
41
The relationship between the phases was elucidated by powder ageing studies, using PXRD,
SHG or Raman analyses. It has been shown that the phases produced by the supercritical routes
(Phase II or a mixture of phases I+II) evolve naturally over time towards the Phase I (stable phase
in ambient conditions), and that the transition kinetics are probably significantly influenced by the
initial proportion of Phase I in the powder. For a pure metastable Phase II, this transition is much
longer.
After this study, one can postulate that for creating Phase II, CO2 molecules are mandatory; it
leads to an inclusion compound in which CO2 molecules are located in channels. These channels
could also guest some solvent molecules coming from crystallization solvent or from water
molecules present in the air. Once included in the cavities of the crystalline structure of Phase II,
the water molecules would stabilize the structure. Thermal DSC and XRD analyses show that these
transitions can be accelerated by heat treatment of the powder.
The results as a whole converge towards the assertion that Phase II, exclusively produced by
CO2-technique, is a metastable phase which evolves towards the stable Phase I fabricated by LAG,
with however a probable more complex landscape of phases since the presence of an intermediate
phase visible at 100°C was revealed.
