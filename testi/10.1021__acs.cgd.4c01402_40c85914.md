# Exploring the Structural Diversity in a Series of Caffeine:Gallic Acid 1:1 Cocrystals with Different Ratios of Solvent and Water: Insight into Intermolecular Interactions in Crystal Packings

DOI: 10.1021/acs.cgd.4c01402

## Abstract

The crystalline structures of five new caffeine and gallic acid cocrystals with a stoichiometry 1:1 (CAF-GAL•0H 2 O, CAF-GAL•0.5H 2 O form III, CAF-GAL•0.75H 2 O, CAF-GAL•1H 2 O•1MeOH and CAF-GAL•2MeOH) but different water and methanol ratios have been solved at low temperatures by single-crystal X-ray diffraction, and the cell parameters were followed as a function of temperature. All these systems contain stacked layers of planar molecules. Perpendicular to these layers linked via weaker interactions (π···π or weak hydrogen bonds), the expansion of the unit cell with temperature always appears relatively high. Inside the layers sustained by stronger hydrogen bonds, the thermal expansion is weaker. Three structures have common dimeric synthons and thus exhibit similar infinite chains of hydrogen bonds mostly perpendicular to each other (CAF-GAL•0H 2 O, CAF-GAL•0.5H 2 O form III, and CAF-GAL•0.75H 2 O), the synthons being different in the two methanol solvates (CAF-GAL•1H 2 O•1MeOH and CAF-GAL•2MeOH). Only two crystals shrink on heating in one direction (it is referred to as uniaxial negative thermal expansion NTE) and these structures are reported to exhibit caffeine disorder (CAF-GAL•0H 2 O, CAF-GAL•0.75H 2 O). In the disolvate (CAF-GAL•1H 2 O•1MeOH), the system shows zero thermal expansion (ZTE). The mechanisms behind these relatively unusual behaviors and the relationship between these solvate structures are discussed with the help of DFT intermolecular energy calculations.

## INTRODUCTION

Crystal engineering is the understanding of intermolecular interactions in crystalline solids and
exploiting this knowledge for the rational design of new crystalline forms with controlled
2
properties 1. In recent decades, efforts have been made to control thermal expansion in various
materials 2. Materials usually experience positive thermal expansion (PTE), expanding with
heating in all directions. Materials with a negative thermal expansion (NTE) in one, two, or even
all three directions have the counterintuitive property of shrinking on heating 3–7. Some materials
experience ZTE or zero thermal expansion: in that case, the temperature has a stable performance
over the temperature range considered. NTE (and ZTE) are generally considered to be rare 4. NTE
is described mainly in a variety of inorganic compounds (the most studied materials being Cu2O 8
or ZrW2O8), 9 but it has also been observed in polymers 10–12 and in water 13. NTE has been reported
in proteins 14 and in small organic molecules of pharmaceutical interest 15–17. NTE has different
applications. For example, forming a material composite with PTE and NTE can lead to a tailored
compound with the desired thermal expansion 7. In the pharmaceutical industry, the thermal
expansion of the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) 's chosen solid form may significantly
impact the formulated pills' integrity over the range of temperatures in manufacturing, storage, or
transport 17.
The underlying mechanisms for NTE are complex, and a general explanation must be found to
predict such behavior. NTE has been interpreted in terms of rigid unit modes (RUM) 18,19, torsional
vibrations in polymers 10, ordering of water molecules 13, and cooperative movements involving
the hydrogen-bond network in methanol monohydrate 20 and small organic molecules such as
(S,S)- octa-3,5-diyin-2,7-diol or tienoxolol 15. Liu et al. proposed a theory based on
thermodynamics and a T-P phase diagram. They stated that the NTE phenomenon in inorganic
compounds originates from high-pressure, small-volume phases with higher entropy with their
configurations present in the stable phase matrix through thermal fluctuations 21. Structural
flexibility is the critical factor for NTE: cooperative movements dominate the effect of chemical
3
bond expansion. The study of thermal expansion may then provide much information about the
intermolecular interactions in a crystal.
Recently, the anisotropy of intermolecular interactions has been measured in cocrystals 22.
Cocrystals are single-phase crystalline solids with at least two distinct neutral molecules in the
same crystalline lattice, these molecules being linked via noncovalent interactions (hydrogen
bonds and van der Waals interactions). In pharmaceutical cocrystals, at least one molecule is of
pharmaceutical interest, the other molecule being picked up in a list of GRAS (generally
recognized as safe) coformers. The increasing interest in cocrystals by the pharmaceutical industry
is because they may have more suitable physicochemical properties, henceforth better
bioavailability or storage properties without altering their pharmacological properties. Caffeine
(CAF) is a model pharmaceutical compound with several polymorphs, all known to be very
sensitive to humidity 23, with the formation of a crystalline monohydrate under high relative
humidity 24 25. Caffeine has a weakly basic imidazole nitrogen (Figure 1); it is known to form
heteromeric synthons, and consequently, it is particularly suited for cocrystallization: a search in
the Cambridge Structural Database (CSD) has led to more than 140 cocrystals or salts with about
115 different coformers 26–28. Moreover, cocrystallization proves to be a remedy for caffeine
hydration in some cases 29. Caffeine generally forms cocrystals with carboxylic acids (and in
particular hydroxybenzoic and dihydroxybenzoic acids) that involve OH···N hydrogen bond
between the COOH group of the acid and the imidazole group of caffeine 26. Among all the
possible coformers, gallic acid (GAL, Figure 1) gives cocrystals with different stoichiometry:
CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form I 30,31 and 3CAF-GAL•6H2O 25. These two hydrates have seen their
crystalline structures solved while CAF-GAL•0H2O 30,31 exists only as powder. In a recent work,
4
our group obtained two other cocrystals (CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form II and CAF-GAL•1.5H2O)
whose structures remained unknown 32.
Figure 1. Molecular structures of gallic acid (GAL) (a) and caffeine (CAF) (b).
This paper reports one of these cocrystal structures: CAF-GAL 1:1 anhydrous, and four
additional structures also containing caffeine and gallic acid 1:1 but with various amounts of water
or methanol molecules in the asymmetric unit. Single crystal X-ray diffraction (SC-XRD) was
used to characterize the temperature behavior. The isobaric thermal expansion tensor was
calculated from SC-XRD measurements at different temperatures and showed ZTE and NTE
phenomena. DFT calculations and synthon analysis were used to interpret their thermal behavior.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials.
Commercial white powders of caffeine CAF (≥ 99.0%) and gallic acid GAL (≥ 97.5%) have
been purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used as provided. Solvent methanol was purchased from
VWR (Normapur, 100%).
5

have been used to identify their crystalline forms as polymorphic form II of caffeine and
polymorphic form II of gallic acid, as stated in a previous article 32.
Synthesis of the Cocrystals.
CAF-GAL•1H2O•1MeOH and CAF-GAL•0.75H2O.
CAF and GAL (in stoichiometry 1:1) were wholly dissolved in methanol at room temperature.
After a few months, translucent single crystals of CAF-GAL•1H2O•1MeOH were obtained and
measured by single-crystal X-ray diffraction. Surprisingly the structure revealed a disolvate
crystalline form with both water and methanol in the asymmetric unit. Another slow evaporation
experiment (in stoichiometry 1:2) has given single crystals of CAF-GAL•0.75H2O.
CAF-GAL•0H2O, CAF-GAL•2MeOH, CAF-GAL•0.5H2O Form III.
Single crystals were obtained in the laboratory.
Single-Crystal X-ray Diffraction (SC-XRD) Experiments and Refinements.
Single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SC-XRD) measurements, with the exception of the CAF-
GAL•0.75H2O, were performed on a Bruker D8 Venture four-axis diffractometer with a Photon II
detector and a Mo K (  = 0.71079 Å) X-ray TRIUMPH monochromator sealed tube source. A
single crystal (Figure S1) was mounted on a glass rod with cyanoacrylate glue for each new
caffeine and gallic acid cocrystal. The crystalline samples were kept at low temperatures during
the experiments: 120 K for CAF-GAL•0H2O and 100 K for the three others. The APEX III
software 33 was used for data collection following strategies including scans along the ω and ϕ
angles. The images were integrated and processed with the SAINT V8.40B software 33. The
6
SADABS-2016/2 program, combined with the APEX III software, was used for absorption
correction and data scaling. The numerical correction method based on crystal face indexing was
applied for all samples except the CAF-GAL anhydrous. The multiscan absorption correction
method was used for the latter due to poor crystal shape. Maximal resolution truncation has been
applied case by case to discard poor-quality data before structure solving and refinement.
Preliminary atomic structure models (Table S1) were solved using the intrinsic phasing method
with APEX III.
In the case of CAF-GAL•0.75H2O, single-crystal X-ray diffraction data have been collected at
100(2) K on the CRISTAL beamline at Synchrotron Soleil, using an Xcalibur Atlas four-circle
diffractometer and equipped with a CCD plate detector, and a beam wavelength set at 0.67163 Å.
A single crystal was mounted on a MiTeGen MicroLoop with grease. Data collection following
strategies including ω and ϕ angle scans, analysis and reduction were performed using the CrysAlis
suite. An empirical absorption correction was applied based on multiple scans of equivalent
reflections, available in the SCALE3 ABSPACK program.
Atomic structure models were refined by least-squares minimization with full matrix using the
SHELXL version 2018 program 34 via the OLEX2 software interface 35. In the first step, hydrogen
atoms were placed geometrically, considered isotropic, and refined using riding models available
in the SHELXL software. Unless otherwise indicated, orientations of the hydroxyl and methyl
groups are freely refined. Treatment of non-H atoms differs between structures depending on the
data quality. In the second step, atom positions were optimized using DFT calculations (see the
section “DFT calculations using CRYSTAL17 and Gaussian09”). This second step has been
applied to the five structures to overcome the difference in experimental crystal structure quality
7
and thus give more meaning to comparing intermolecular interactions in the different crystal
packings.
In the case of the cocrystal disolvate water/methanol, referred to as CAF-GAL•1H2O•1MeOH,
the subatomic resolution of the data set (limit resolution of 0.65 Å) allowed refining all heavy
atoms with anisotropic atomic displacement parameters (ADPs) and locating all H atoms in the
Fourier difference map. For the cocrystal solvate methanol, termed CAF-GAL•2MeOH, and the
cocrystal hemihydrate form III, termed CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form III, the data sets collected were
of limited quality with a limit resolution near 1.0 Å. Thus, all heavy atoms have been considered
isotropic. No clear evidence of structural disorder was noticeable. Hydrogen atoms of caffeine
molecules were localized based on Fourier difference map. However, hydroxyl H atoms on the
trihydroxybenzene moiety of gallic acid molecules could not all be oriented, plus H atoms of
methanol molecules in the case of CAF-GAL•2MeOH. Their orientations have been deduced from
their local environments to optimize the intermolecular H-bond network. For the cocrystals, CAF-
GAL•0H2O and CAF-GAL•0.75H2O, the oxygen atom of the 1/2 water, labeled #2, is on a 2-fold
rotation axis. In both cases, the heavy atoms of nondisordered moieties were considered
anisotropic and freely refined, while configurations of disordered caffeine molecules were handled
as isotropic. Moreover, distance equivalence restraints and ADP equality constraints were applied
between the caffeine configurations to ease model refinement convergence. The Fourier residual
maps unequivocally showed the positions of H atoms, except the methyl-group orientations of
disordered caffeine molecules due to the superimposing of both caffeine configurations.

DFT Calculations Using CRYSTAL17 and Gaussian09.
As mentioned in the section “Single-Crystal X-ray Diffraction (SC-XRD) experiments and
refinements”, in four of the crystal structures reported in this work, orientations of hydroxyl and
methyl groups could not be deduced unambiguously from Fourier residual maps. The H-atom
positions were adjusted based on the local intermolecular environment to optimize the hydrogen
bond network. In general, the inability of single-crystal X-ray diffraction to properly locate
hydrogen atoms is well-known. It is worth remembering that, due to quality discrepancies between
data sets, the structures were refined considering different atomic thermal displacement model
levels. Moreover, two of these structures, CAF-GAL•0H2O and CAF-GAL•0.75H2O, present
caffeine disorder which could not be properly solved without structural restrains. Therefore, before
exploring and comparing the intermolecular interactions inside crystal packings, theoretical
calculations were run to optimize the positions of all atoms, keeping fixed cell parameters. Periodic
DFT computations were undertaken using CRYSTAL17 39 with the theoretical framework
B3LYP-D3(BJ)/pob-TZVP to account for the crystalline environment.
B3LYP-D3(BJ), that is the B3LYP functional corrected with the Grimme’s D3 dispersion 40,41
and Becke–Johnson (BJ) damping 42, allows taking into account the weak interactions, including
van der Waals dispersive interactions. The performance of the B3LYP-D3 functional has been
demonstrated both at the molecular level 43 and in molecular crystals 44. In the case of a structural
disorder, both possible conformations were handled separately, and thus two non-disordered
crystal structures were processed. Such a preliminary step is essential because the H-bond network
can be assumed to play a crucial role in intermolecular interactions. The description of the H-bond
network in crystal packing was performed starting from optimized structures.
10
To discuss the structural disorders observed in crystals CAF-GAL•0H2O and CAF-
GAL•0.75H2O, intermolecular interaction energies between close neighbors were estimated by
theoretical calculations. The structural geometries of neighboring molecule pairs were taken from
the full-atom optimized crystal structures. To obtain a reasonable estimate of interaction energies,
energy estimation was carried out by performing single-point calculation using the theoretical level
DFT/M06-2X/cc-PVTZ, 45 available from the Gaussian09 software 46. M06-2X, that is a meta-
GGA approximation based functional, uses parametrized medium-range electron correlations,
which allows one to describe the dispersion interactions. It is considered the most reliable among
Minnesota Density 06 functionals 47. Additionally, the calculation of such energy using
CrystalExplorer and B3LYP/6-31G* has been performed. The two functionals, B3LYP-D3 and
M06-2X, are popular and have been both used in several studies, either for comparison 48,49 or a
complementary way: B3LYP-3D for structural optimization and M06-2X for calculations of
electronic properties 50,51.

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Description of Crystal Structures.
11
In the literature, two single-crystal structures of cocrystals CAF and GAL have been published:
CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form I 30 and 3CAF-GAL•6H2O 25. A third formula, anhydrous CAF-
GAL•0H2O has been characterized by PXRD 30. In 2022 32, our group discovered two new forms.
The first crystal, CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form II, was obtained from the coprecipitation of CAF and
GAL in water at room temperature, and the second, CAF-GAL•1.5H2O, was obtained from a laser-
induced nucleation method. Both crystalline forms are dehydrated when heated into CAF-
GAL•0H2O, which melts at 238 °C or 511 K 32. Unfortunately, we did not obtain single crystals
of a suitable size for crystal structure determination. Therefore, the crystalline structures of these
two forms remained unknown.
In this work, we were able to obtain a single crystal of anhydrous CAF-GAL•0H2O, and we
discovered four new cocrystals: CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form III, CAF-GAL•0.75H2O, CAF-
GAL•1H2O•1MeOH and CAF-GAL•2MeOH. Their crystalline structures were determined by
single-crystal diffraction and are presented in this paper (Table S1). In all five of these new
crystalline forms, the stoichiometry CAF:GAL is 1:1 but with different ratios of water or methanol
molecules. Both molecules are neutral in every crystalline phase. The asymmetric units are
depicted in Figure 2.

CAF-GAL•0H2O.
The cocrystal anhydrous form, CAF-GAL•0H2O is monoclinic with a space group C 2/c (Table
S1). The asymmetric unit of anhydrous CAF-GAL consists of one gallic acid and one disordered
caffeine (Figure 2a). The caffeine molecule is disordered with two distinct orientations. It has been
modeled with 50% occupancy, which seems typical for the caffeine molecule, and has been
referred to as parallel and antiparallel stacking of caffeine 54. We will refer to this disorder as
orientations OA and OB in this article, and each orientation involves different kinds of hydrogen
bonds in the system (Figure S2). The molecules CAF and GAL are packed in layers. In a layer,
pairs of molecules GAL related by an inversion center and interacting via a double H-bond O5G-
13
H5G···O6G, are surrounded by molecules CAF (Figure S2a). Each molecule CAF interacts with
three molecules GAL via strong O-H···O/N type H-bonds. Both CAF orientations share one H-
bond, O1G-H1G···O2C, while the two others differ with the caffeine flipping. Despite the nature
of the acceptor (nitrogen or oxygen) change, the geometries are very similar with a difference of
distance Donor···Acceptor of 0.1 Å or less (Table S2). In the crystal packing, near-neighbor layers
are related by a translation along 1 2 ⁄ 𝑎+ 1 2 ⁄ 𝑏 (Figure S2b). Between near-neighboring layers,
some molecules GAL and CAF interact via π···π stacking (Figure S2c).
CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form III.
CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form III crystallizes in the P 21/c space group with two crystallographically
inequivalent caffeine molecules, two inequivalent gallic acid molecules, and only one water
molecule (Figure 2b). Consequently, the formula in the asymmetric unit is 2CAF, 2GAL, H2O.
The CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form I crystallizes in C2/c, and exhibits only one caffeine and one gallic
acid in the asymmetric unit 30. No disorder was observed in the two hemihydrates. In CAF-
GAL•0.5H2O form III, there are also parallel layers of molecules but organized by pairs of two
crystallographically equivalent layers. Thus, the crystal packing (Table S3) is a stack of double
layers of molecules CAF1 and GAL1, and of molecules CAF2 and GAL2 (Figure S3a). In each
layer made of molecules CAF1 and GAL1, or CAF2 and GAL2, the same network of H-bonds can
be observed. Each caffeine molecule forms H-bonds with gallic acid ones and reciprocally (Figures
S3b,c). Double layers interact via π···π stacking between molecules CAF and GAL. Water
molecules are only located between double layers, and each of them creates four H-bonds
exclusively with GAL molecules (Figures S3d). As a water molecule is located on a glide c mirror
perpendicular to the axis b, its H-bonds are two-by-two identical.
14
A close look at the asymmetric unit shows a quasi-symmetry type glide c perpendicular to the
axis a, between the pairs (CAF1, GAL1) and (CAF2, GAL2) (Figure S4b). The geometry of the
strong H-bonds observed in the crystal packing supports this conclusion, with almost the same H-
bonds for the pairs (CAF1, GAL1) and (CAF2, GAL2). However, such symmetry is incompatible
with a crystal system monoclinic, as such symmetry would impose a β angle of 90° and a space
group P ccn. Figure S5 compares the PXRD pattern calculated for CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form III
with the calculated one of form I and the experimental pattern of form II proving that it is indeed
a new polymorph of the CAF-GAL hemihydrate cocrystal.
CAF-GAL•0.75H2O.
CAF-GAL•0.75H2O also crystallizes in the C 2/c space group. There are two caffeine molecules
and two gallic acid molecules in the asymmetric unit, plus two water molecules. Nonetheless, one
of the latter is on a symmetry element, leading to a stoichiometry 2:2:1.5 (Figure 2c). The crystal
packing (Table S4) is a stack of corrugated layers going two by two: one double layer of molecules
CAF1 and GAL1, and then one double layer of molecules CAF2 and GAL2 (Figure S6b).
However, in this case, the water molecules are inside the double layers while they are between
them in the crystal CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form III. In the double layer CAF1-GAL1, the water
molecule WAT1 is on a 2-fold axis.
In both double layers, while the H-bond networks are distinct, each caffeine molecule still forms
three H-bonds with gallic acid ones and reciprocally (Figure S6c,d). The two double layers do not
contain the same number of water molecules: a half water for one CAF and one GAL in the layer
#1 and one molecule in the second. The hemihydrated double layer CAF1-GAL1 is not disordered
and can be compared with the crystal packing CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form III. In a layer, the
15
molecules CAF and GAL share in both cases the same orientation and H-bonds in their
environment (Figure S6e). However, between the two layers, water molecules have different
orientations. The monohydrated double layer CAF2-GAL2 is distinct with each water molecule
WAT2 interacting with three molecules GAL2 instead of four (Figure S6f). Like in the case of
CAF-GAL•0H2O, the caffeine CAF2 is disordered with two orientations which leads to two
potential series of H-bonds with its close environment but with almost the same H-bond geometry
whatever the acceptor atom type (Table S4). Surprisingly, the parallel-antiparallel caffeine
disorder presents occupancies of 78.8(4) and 21.2(4) % instead of the typical 50% occupancies for
the caffeine molecules.
CAF-GAL•1H2O•1MeOH.
CAF-GAL•1H2O•1MeOH crystallizes in the triclinic P–1 space group, with only one caffeine
and one gallic acid molecule in the asymmetric unit, one methanol molecule, and one additional
water molecule, presumably coming from the atmospheric vapor (Figure 2d). No disorder is
observed. Parallel layers of molecules interacting by π‧‧‧π stacking can be seen (Table S5). Two
neighbor layers are related by central inversion (Figure S7b). The presence of water and methanol
molecules makes the interaction pattern within a plane unique. The molecules GAL form dimers
involved in double H-bonds via their carboxylic groups, resulting from the central inversion
(Figure S7c). Each molecule GAL makes a strong H-bond with only one molecule CAF, and
reciprocally. Solvent molecules go by water-methanol pairs and are localized inside columns along
the axis c (Figure S7d). They have a major role as intermediates first between molecules CAF
inside a layer and second between molecules CAF and GAL from two close layers (Figure S7e).

Crystal Packing of Hydrates.
Comparison of Crystal Packings
A strict comparison between the already reported CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form I 30 and the newly
found form III is quite enlightening. Indeed, their crystal structures are stacks of CAF-GAL layers.
In both crystal forms, layers are similar, sharing the same CAF and GAL orientations and H-bond
network (Figure S10). Water molecules are involved in one interlayer space out of two, making
appear water-free double layers of CAF and GAL separated by water molecules. Each double layer
consists of two layers related by crystallographic symmetries. In both hemihydrate forms, these
symmetries are the same with the 21 screw axis and inversion operations, which leads to similar
double layers. The water interlayer space is where hemihydrates differ. In CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form
I, close neighbor double layers are related by symmetries: inversion centers and 2-fold axes.
Moreover, each water molecule is located on a two-fold axis. In CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form III, there
is no crystallographic symmetry between the two neighboring double layers. Nonetheless, as
previously explained, the asymmetric unit of the hemihydrate form III presents a
noncrystallographic glide c type symmetry (Figure S8e). The quasi-symmetry glide c occurs
between double layers. In that respect, the two hemihydrate forms primarily differ in the way
double layers pile up (Figure S10). This seems to be related to the orientation of water molecules
in their interlayer sites. When a water molecule interacts with two molecules of GAL from one
layer via two H-bonds: one as the H-bond donor and the other as the acceptor, its second hydrogen
21
atom can take two orientations to form two H-bonds with molecules of GAL from the other
neighboring layer (Figure S8e).
As described previously, the cocrystal CAF-GAL•0.75H2O has quite peculiar packing with an
alternation of hemihydrated double layers and monohydrated ones, giving a Neapolitan cake
aspect. A partial comparison with the hemihydrate forms can be made by considering only the
structure of hemihydrated double layers in the cocrystal CAF-GAL•0.75H2O. As already
mentioned, in a hemihydrated double layer, each layer shares the same layout as in the hemihydrate
form III, and hence as in form I. Moreover, there are crystallographic symmetries between the two
layers. They are in fact related by the very same symmetries: inversion centers and 2-fold axes, as
in the crystal CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form I (Figure S10). Water molecules are once again located on
the 2-fold axes. It is relevant in that respect to note that the cocrystals CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form I
and CAF-GAL•0.75H2O form share the same space group, C 2/c.
Theoretical Investigation of Crystallographic Disorder.
Structural disorder of a caffeine molecule has been observed in the asymmetric units of two new
cocrystals: CAF-GAL•0H2O and CAF-GAL•0.75H2O. The disordered caffeine has two
orientations, labeled OA and OB, related to each other by a 180° flipping. DFT calculations were
performed to discuss how such molecular disorder may be possible without impairing crystal
packing. As explained in the section “DFT calculations using CRYSTAL17 and Gaussian09”, for
each disordered crystal structure, theoretical calculations have been performed considering two
nondisordered structures: one per caffeine orientation. For each crystal structure, full atom
optimization was first performed by a DFT periodic calculation using CRYSTAL17. Dimers of
close neighboring molecules were then extracted from the crystal packing. Lastly, pairwise
22
interaction energies were estimated by DFT/M06-2X calculation with the BSSE correction. The
list of the major intermolecular interactions inside the crystal packing of CAF-GAL•0H2O is
displayed in Table S2. For an easier understanding, these interactions are illustrated with the
corresponding interaction energies in Figure 4.
In Figures 5a,b illustrating the main intermolecular interactions in the crystal CAF-GAL•0H2O,
the CAF molecule shows significant changes in its interaction energies in the local environment.
The molecule interacts with four molecules GAL: three via H-bonds, plus one significant contact.

of Miller indices (210) (Figure S7). Identical parallel layers are linked via both water and methanol
molecules, and only weak π···π stacking interactions.
The eigenvectors (𝑒1; 𝑒2; 𝑒3) are not related to the cell vectors (𝑎; 𝑏; 𝑐) as the crystal system is
triclinic. A close look at the crystal packing shows that the plane (𝑒1; 𝑒2) is almost parallel to the
Miller (110) plane. A view of the molecules in this plane reveals the probable role of the solvent
molecules in the near-zero or zero thermal expansion (ZTE) along 𝑒1 (Figure 11).
The maximal positive thermal expansion (PTE) expected along 𝑒3 may induce displacements of
the water and methanol molecules, with a shortening of the water-methanol H-bond projected on
the plane (𝑒1; 𝑒2) as illustrated with blue arrows in Figure 11. This may compensate for the local
expansion of H-bonds such as synthon IX in the Miller (110) plane (illustrated with green double
arrow in Figure 11) and explain the weak thermal expansion along 𝑒1.

## CONCLUSIONS

This work reports the crystalline structures of five new caffeine and gallic acid cocrystals with
a 1:1 stoichiometry with different solvations: CAF-GAL•0H2O, CAF-GAL•0.5H2O III, CAF-
39
GAL•0.75H2O, CAF-GAL•1H2O•1MeOH and CAF-GAL•2MeOH. The main conclusions are as
follows.
(i)  The analysis of their crystal packings and identification of the dimeric synthons involved
in their hydrogen bond networks highlight their specificities and remarkable structural
similarities in some cases. The cocrystals CAF-GAL•0H2O, CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form III
and CAF-GAL•0.75H2O share the same key synthons III, V and VIII. The two other
structures differ by the involvement of solvent molecules in the intralayer interactions,
leading to new synthons.
(ii)  The comparison of the new hemihydrate crystalline forms, CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form III,
with the already reported CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form I, reveals in both, stacks of double
CAF - GAL layers with, between them, water molecules. Their double layers are very
similar with the same hydrogen bond network and synthons. Their structures only differ
in the way their double layers pile up.
(iii)  The cocrystal CAF-GAL•0.75H2O has a remarkably “Neapolitan cake” structure with a
stack of two distinct types of hydrated double CAF - GAL layers interacting via π···π
stacking: one hemihydrated similar to the structure of CAF-GAL•0.5H2O form I, and the
other monohydrated.
(iv)  Four thermal expansions have been measured by single crystal X-ray diffraction for
CAF-GAL•0H2O,  CAF-GAL•0.5H2O  III,  CAF-GAL•0.75H2O  and  CAF-
GAL•1H2O•1MeOH. Two of them, CAF-GAL•0H2O and CAF-GAL•0.75H2O, exhibit
negative thermal expansions, and a third one, CAF-GAL•1H2O•1MeOH, a zero-thermal
expansion. In the cases of CAF-GAL•0H2O and CAF-GAL•1H2O•1MeOH, possible
mechanisms have been proposed based on the analysis of dimeric synthons and the in
40
silico calculations of their interaction energies. For the remaining case with NTE, the
structure of CAF-GAL•0.75H2O is very interesting but also very complex due to its
“Neapolitan cake” structure and so only a partial explanation has been suggested. For
this reason, further investigation would be necessary to identify mechanisms behind its
thermal expansion behavior.
