# A Peptoid-like Approach Led to Lactam–Lactam Dimer Formation in 2-Hydroxy-N-alkyl-N-phenyl-nicotinamides and Their Polymorphism and Solvatomorphism

DOI: 10.1021/acsomega.3c04980

## Abstract

AbstractFour 2-hydroxy-N-alkyl-N-phenyl-nicotinamides (1–4) were synthesized, and their crystal structures were analyzed to investigate the effect of substitution on their crystal packing of N-phenyl-2-hydroxynicotinanilides. In these compounds, substituents were introduced on the amide N, leading to a peptoid-like structure. One solvent-free form and two hydrates were harvested for compound 1, and one anhydrous form and one hydrate were obtained for compound 2. Polymorphism was observed in compounds 3 and 4. The molecules were found to be in the keto form rather than the enol tautomer. Because of steric effects, the molecules took on an E configuration, leading to a hairpin-like geometry. A lactam–lactam dimer synthon was formed in all solvent-free structures, and a tetramer motif was observed for the first time. Dehydration of the two hydrates of 1 and the hydrate of 2 led to their respective solvent-free form. Phase transition between the polymorphs was revealed in compound 3. Theoretical calculations, including conformational energy evaluation, hydrate forming propensity assessment, and lattice energy appraisal, were performed to provide a reasonable explanation for the keto tautomer and the formation of the hydrates of compound 1.

## Introduction

1.
Crystal engineering comprises the understanding of intermolecular interactions in the context of crystal packing and its application in the design of new solids with desired physical and chemical properties.1,2 In recent years, crystal engineering has been developing rapidly.3 The most commonly used design strategy in crystal engineering employs supramolecular synthons, which are the smallest structural units in supramolecules. The concept of synthon was first proposed by Corey to describe “the key structural characteristics of target molecules in organic synthesis” in 19674 and was extended by Desiraju as “a supramolecular structural unit formed or assembled by known or imagined synthetic operations involving intermolecular interactions”.5 There are two types of supramolecular synthons, i.e., homosynthon and heterosynthon. The homosynthon consists of two identical complementary functional groups, such as carboxylic acid or amide, whereas the heterosynthon involves interactions between two different complementary functional groups, such as acid and pyridine, acid and amide, hydroxyl and amide, hydroxyl and pyridine, etc.6−9 Supramolecular synthons are descriptors of an assembly to describe a noncovalent assembly.10−13 It is established that small changes in the chemical structure can profoundly affect the crystal packing and further change the physicochemical properties of organic compounds. Consequently, substitution plays a significant role in the crystal packing of crystalline materials of high-value compounds such as pharmaceuticals.14−17
In two previous studies, we investigated the solid-state structural properties of a series of N-phenyl-2-hydroxynicotinanilides (PHNAs)18,19 (Figure 1), compounds with both amide and lactam functionalities. These compounds have potential as anti-inflammatory drugs,20−22 PI3Kα inhibitors,23 and full CB2 receptor inverse agonists.24 In our earlier studies, we had investigated the role of the aniline moiety on the crystal packing. In both studies, a variety of substituents were introduced on the aniline ring by changing the position of substitution and type of substituents. Depending on the substituents, there were two consequences on the packing of compound. The compounds having less steric repulsion between the aromatic rings provided a planar conformation, and the ones having significant steric repulsion, such as the one possessing ortho-substituted aniline, adopted a twisted conformer. The conformational difference consequentially led to the packing variations in the crystals of these compounds: most of the planar molecules self-associate to form lactam–lactam dimers (LLDs); occasionally, they also form a lactam-amide catemer (LAC) (Figure 2), exhibiting synthon polymorphism;25−27 for nonplanar molecules, LAC was exclusively observed. Hence, in such examples, the steric effect played an important role in determining the hydrogen bond consequences of those compounds, but it was not the sole factor.
In this work, we investigated the structural properties of a new series of PHNAs. In these compounds, substitution was restricted to the amide N (Scheme 1), similar to the structure of peptoids. The replacement of the N–H with an N-alkyl group may have two consequences: disruption of the conjugation of the two aromatic rings and elimination of one hydrogen-bond donor. We wondered what would happen in the solid state. Would the keto form still be favored, as in the two previous series of compounds, or would the LAC motif prevail or the LLD predominate? What role might steric hindrance play? Could chain length make a difference? To answer these questions, crystals were grown in a variety of apolar, polar aprotic, and polar protic solvents. Solvatomorphism28−31 was observed in compounds 1 and 2, and polymorphism was revealed in compounds 3 and 4. The dominant synthon observed in all four molecules is the LLD. The phase behavior of these systems was also investigated. Theoretical studies including conformational energy evaluation, hydrate forming propensity assessment, and lattice energy calculation were performed to provide certain insight into the formation of these systems.

## Computational Details
2.4.
Molecular structures for the tautomers of 2 (R = −CH2CH3) were optimized with the DEF2-SVP basis set and functional B3LYP(D3BJ) using ORCA5.0.1,41 and then the free energy at 298 K was calculated with the larger basis set DEF2-TZVPP. The hydrate formation propensity of different molecules was assessed with COSMO-RS.42 The lattice energies of the crystal structures of the four compounds were calculated to explain the hydrate formation in compounds 1 and 2 and the polymorphism in compound 4. For lattice energy calculation, the crystal structures were optimized, and energies of the structures were obtained. The calculation was conducted using VASP 6.3.43 The functional was OPTPBE-VDW,44 the pseudopotential was PAW, and the energy cutoff for plane-wave-basis set was 520 eV. The k-point was generated by the KSPACING method with Rk of 0.5/Å. The tendency of these four molecules to form hydrates was simulated. In addition, to illuminate the influence of the alkyl groups on the crystal formation of these molecules, the molecules in 1-A, 1-H-I, and 1-H-II were replaced with molecules of 2, 3, and 4, respectively; the corresponding virtual crystal structures were obtained; and their lattice energies were calculated along with those of the real crystal structures. For lattice energy calculation, tblite45 with the GFT-xTB method46 was used, and the lattice parameters and atom positions of all structures were optimized with the bfgs method in the ASE package.47

## Crystallization Results
3.1.
Three forms, one anhydrate (1-A) and two hydrates (1-H-I and 1-H-II), were obtained for 1, and two forms were harvested for 2 (2-I and 2-H), 3 (3-I and 3-II), and 4 (4-I and 4-II). Representative crystals for each form are shown in Figure 3. The crystallographic data of compounds 1–4 are listed in Table 1.
## Structural Properties
3.2.
The asymmetric units of 1-A, 1-H-I, 1-H-II, 2-I, 3-I, 4-I, and 4-II consist of two (A, red; B, blue), two (one host molecule and the other water), two (one host molecule and one water), two (A, red; B, blue), one, and two (A, red; B, blue) molecules, respectively. All of the host molecules are highly twisted, as indicated by the dihedral angles between their two aromatic rings (Table 2). The nonplanar conformation, however, is different from that of the previous compounds, which is due to rotation about the Namide–Caniline bond. A hairpin-like geometry48 is observed due to the rotation of both Clactam–Camide and Namide–Caniline, leaving both aromatic rings on the same side of the amide N–C bond (E configuration). The hairpin geometry is further stabilized mainly by intramolecular π–π stacking, which is observed between the two aromatic rings in 1–4.
## Compound 1
3.2.1.
In 1-A, two A molecules (red) pair up to form a homodimer based on the LLD synthon, with hydrogen bond parameters of 2.832 Å for bond length and 168.86° for bond angle. The homodimer is flanked by one B molecule (blue) on either side through a hydrogen bond between the NH of the lactam of B and C=O of the lactam of A (Figure 4). The hydrogen bond has a bond length of 2.844 Å and bond angle of 173.38°. The formation of a unique tetramer is a first in these compounds.
In 1-H-I, the host molecules are no longer directly associated with each other. Instead, each host molecule forms three hydrogen bonds with three water molecules, once as a hydrogen-bond donor (HBD) and twice as a hydrogen-bond acceptor (HBA). Each water molecule also forms three hydrogen bonds with three host molecules, once as HBA and twice as HBD. The water molecules are linkers for the host molecules, acting as HBD to one lactam C=O and as HBA to the other lactam NH to connect two host molecules; thus, two water molecules and two host molecules form an R44(12) tetramer. The remaining OH of the water molecule hydrogen bonds with the remaining C=O of the amide, establishing a netlike packing network (Figure 5). The hydrogen bonding between the NH in the pyridine ring and O of water has parameters of 2.784 Å and 157.59°. The hydrogen bond between the OH of water and C=O of amide has parameters of 2.822 Å and 178.36°. The hydrogen bond between the OH of water and C=O in the pyridine ring has parameters of 2.739 Å and 164.28°.
The packing of 1-H-II is drastically different from that of 1-H-I. Four water molecules self-associate to form an R44(8) tetramer with each molecule providing one HBD and one HBA. The additional HBD, i.e., OH hydrogen, bonds with the C=O of the amide. The host molecules themselves hydrogen bond through lactam NH and C=O, forming a catemer motif. The remaining C=O from the amide hydrogen bonds with a water molecule (Figure 6). The hydrogen bond of the water tetramer has bond parameters of 2.828 Å for the bond length and 124.90° for the bond angle. The hydrogen bond between the OH of water and the C=O of the amide has parameters of 2.716 Å and 104.94°. The hydrogen bond between the NH in the pyridine ring of one molecule and the C=O in the pyridine ring of the other molecule has parameters of 2.777 Å and 176.03°.
## Compounds 2 and 3
3.2.2.
Two crystal forms were discovered for compounds 2 and 3, but only one crystal structure was determined for each compound. In 2-I, the two molecules form a heterogeneous LLD through N–H···O=C hydrogen bonding (Figure 7). When the NH of molecule A (red) acts as HBD and the O=C of molecule B (blue) acts as HBA, the hydrogen-bond length and angle are 2.744 Å and 169.18°. When the NH of molecule B acts as the HBD and the O=C of molecule A acts as the HBA, the hydrogen-bond length and angle are 2.823 Å and 118.65°. The molecules in 3-I form homodimers based on LLD, with hydrogen bond parameters of 2.746 Å and 175.01°.
The crystals of 2-H and 3-II were of poor quality, preventing structure determination. The identity of 2-H was confirmed by NMR, giving a water/compound ratio of 2:1 (Figure S14).
## Compound 4
3.2.3.
The two molecules of 4-I pair up to form an A–B heterodimer (Figure 8a) based on the LLD, with hydrogen-bond parameters of 2.769 Å for bond length and 172.32° for bond angle for C=O(A)···NH(B) and 2.919 Å and 163.08° for C=O(B)···NH(A).
The molecules of 4-II form homodimers (Figure 8b) based on LLD with hydrogen-bond parameters of 2.749 Å and 174.60°, similar to 3-I.
## Phase Behavior
3.3.
To study the phase behavior of the crystal forms, first, phase-pure samples were sought, and the purity of each crystal form was demonstrated by their PXRD patterns, except for 1-H-II that seemed to be a mixture of all three forms (Figures S1–S4). Then, the phase behavior of the crystal forms of 1–4 was studied by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and thermogravimetric analysis. Results are listed in Figure 9. For 1-A, only one melting peak, having an onset temperature of 152.8 °C, is observed on the DSC trace, which corresponds to melting. Both 1-H-I and 1-H-II display two thermal events with the first one having onset temperatures of 70.6 and 76.4 °C, respectively, corresponding to the loss of water and the second having onset temperatures of 152.6 and 151.9 °C, respectively, resulting from the melting of the crystals upon dehydration. The dehydration of 1-H-I and 1-H-II was further investigated by TGA and PXRD (Figures S5 and S6). For 2-I, only one peak (melting) with an onset temperature of 152.8 °C is observed on the DSC trace. 2-H, however, displays two thermal events with the first one with an onset temperature of 114.7 °C (dehydration), leading to the formation of 2-I, and the second one with an onset temperature of 146.6 °C. The transformation of 2-H into 2-I by thermal treatment is confirmed by PXRD (Figure S8). The dehydration of 2-H is also confirmed by TGA (Figure S7). For 3-I, only one melting peak with an onset temperature of 172.6 °C is observed on the DSC trace. Meanwhile, 3-II displays two thermal events: one with an onset temperature of 160.9 °C and the second with an onset temperature of 172.2 °C. PXRD shows that the first thermal event is a phase transition into 3-I (Figure S9). For 4-I, only one thermal event with an onset temperature of 155.7 °C is observed. 4-II also displays only one thermal event, with an onset temperature of 153.2 °C, due to melting of the sample. A DSC heating–cooling cycle was also performed for all eight samples. During cooling, no crystallization peak was observed for any sample (Figures S23–S31).
## Computation Results
3.4.
The free energies of the enol and keto forms of 2 were calculated at 298 K. The enol tautomer has a higher free energy than the keto tautomer (about 6.5 kJ/mol). Thus, the keto form of 2 is more stable than the enol form, which is consistent with the experimental results.
Taking the optimized conformers of single molecules as inputs, the hydrate forming propensity of different molecules was calculated with COSMO-RS (Table 3). Molecule 3 has the highest hydrate forming propensity, whereas that of molecule 1 is the lowest, which indicates the lowest probability of a hydrate crystal formation for molecule 1. This is opposite to the experimental results. Molecular conformers from the crystal structures were then used as inputs. Here, the hydrate forming propensity of molecule 3 was still the highest, but that of molecule 1 was next followed by molecule 2. Although this result is relatively more consistent with the experimental results, there is still a discrepancy between the predicted and experimental results, which means other factors must also be in play.
We took a step further to calculate the lattice energies of the experimental crystal structures (Table 4). As we can see, the lattice energies of molecule 1 hydrates are much larger than the anhydrate (>30 kJ/mol, > 12 kbT), which means that the hydrates are much more stable than the anhydrate. When there is water in the crystallization solvent, even if only a marginal amount of it, the final crystal product is likely to be a hydrate. As the interactions between the molecules in crystals are dominated by hydrogen bonds, hydrogen bonds contribute predominantly to the lattice energy. The magnitude of the lattice energy reveals the strength of hydrogen bonds between molecules. As seen, the hydrogen bonds between the molecules of 1 are the weakest, and they are a lot weaker than those of the other molecules. But in the hydrates, the number of hydrogen bonds between the host molecules and water is far higher than in the anhydrate, and the number makes up for the strength of hydrogen bonds. This is the case for the solvatomorphic system of molecule 1. Moreover, the substituent in molecule 1 is the smallest, and there is little steric hindrance between molecules, which provides a reasonable explanation for the tetramer motif formation in compound 1. The lattice energies of the two forms of 4 are nearly identical, which are in agreement with their close melting points.
When the molecules in the crystal structures of 1 are replaced with 2, 3, and 4, which have longer side chains, the crystal packing of the optimized structures for 2-Ah (h = hypothetical), 3-Ah and 2-H-Ih, 3-H-Ih is similar to that of 1. But differences do exist between them, especially for 2-Ah and 3-Ah. In structure 1-A, there is some void (red rectangle) between molecules viewed vertical to the screen, but structures 2-Ah and 3-Ah appear to be more crowded. Moreover, in the middle of the packing plane (yellow square), the atom positions of molecules are also quite different. The structure of 4-Ah is dramatically different from those of the other three, indicating a different crystal packing pattern. This is also the case for 1-H-I and the corresponding hypothetical hydrates 2, 3, and 4 (Figure 10).
The lattice energies of the virtual crystal structures obtained by substituting the molecules in 1-A, 1-H-I, and 1–H-II with molecules of 2, 3, and 4, together with those of the real crystal structures calculated with tblite with the GFT-xTB method, are listed in Table 5.
As can be seen from the lattice energies of 1-H-I and hypothetical crystals resulting from substitution of 1 with molecules of 2, 3, and 4, the lattice energies decrease as the alkyl chain increases, which suggests that the structures become less stable due to alkyl chain elongation. The increase of side chain length in the compounds may cause stronger repulsive forces between molecules in the crystal structures. Moreover, the alkyl chains are hydrophobic, and they may weaken the hydrogen bonds between the molecules and water.
For 1-H-II and the corresponding hypothetical crystals, the length of the alkyl chains has a more significant influence on the crystal structures. Although the 2-H-II structure seems to be reasonable, the 3-H-II and 4-H-II structures cannot even be optimized, which means that they are not stable structures. So, when the alkyl group on the nitrogen is longer, hydration formation is more difficult.

## Conclusions

4.
Four PHNAs with the H on the amide N replaced with alkyl groups were synthesized, and their crystal forms were investigated. Although the molecules can exist in two tautomeric forms, i.e., enol and keto, due to the lower energy of the keto form as confirmed by conformational energy calculations, all the molecules were found to be keto tautomers. Because the amide NH is replaced with NR (R = alkyl), the molecules have an E conformation, with the two aromatic rings on the same side of the amide bond, leading to a hairpin-like geometry. The twisted conformation of these molecules did not lead to LAC synthon formation because of the elimination of the hydrogen bond donor on the amide N. Instead, the LLD is mainly observed, although with the exception in compound 1, which showed a unique tetramer motif consisting of an LLD core flanked by two additional hydrogen bonds. A diverse solid-state behavior was observed for this series of compounds: polymorphism was observed in compounds 3 and 4, and hydrate formation was present in compounds 1 and 2. The phase behavior study revealed a phase transition between the two forms of 3, and dehydration of the two hydrates of 1 and hydrate of 2 led to their respective anhydrous forms. By constructing hypothetical hydates of compounds 2, 3, and 4 and calculating and comparing the lattice energies of these virtual and real structures, a reasonable explanation was provided for the formation of the hydrates of compound 1 and the nearly identical melting points of the two forms of 4. This combination of experimental and theoretical studies can be used to investigate analogues of N-phenyl-2-hydroxynicotinanilides such as N-phenyl-4-hydroxynicotinanilides and N-phenyl-6-hydroxynicotinanilides, and the process is currently under way.
